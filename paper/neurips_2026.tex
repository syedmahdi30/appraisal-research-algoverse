\begin{abstract}
Interpretability tools are used to explain what a vision-language model does with its inputs, but a tool that localizes an effect will localize whatever the measurement puts in front of it. We apply three standard tools to one cross-modal phenomenon and report where each does and does not license a conclusion. The phenomenon is a valence conflict: EMOTIC photographs paired with contradicting one-sentence contexts, where flipping a single valence word with the photograph and event held fixed moves \qwen{}'s judgment four to five times farther when the word is negative, on all six tested pairs. Two claims survive. A ridge probe trained only on text transfers to image-conditioned states ($\rho=+0.510$), and activation patching localizes states sufficient to restore each cue: downstream text positions restore $88$--$93\%$ of the context difference in \gemma{}, while the image's own valence is recovered from image positions early ($100\%$, layers 0--12) and from text positions late ($63\%$, layers 18--28). Two failures qualify them. Localization is readout-dependent: where both our readouts are valid they give non-overlapping intervals in opposite directions about which token group dominates. And the cross-model conclusion was an artifact of a scoring choice --- evaluating only the first token of a multi-token emotion label manufactured a null in one model and reversed the categorical ordering in another, dissolving an apparent architectural boundary. Localization evidence supported the mechanism claim here and not the generalization claim, and the difference was invisible until the measurement changed.
\end{abstract}
\else
\begin{abstract}
Vision-language systems increasingly read images of people alongside text that a user supplies, and the two can disagree. When they disagree about emotional valence, does the model treat a positive and a negative sentence as equally strong? We pair EMOTIC photographs with one-sentence contexts and measure how a conflicting context shifts the model's emotion judgment away from a neutral-context baseline. In the most controlled condition we hold the photograph and the described event fixed and flip a single valence word (won$\leftrightarrow$lost, wonderful$\leftrightarrow$devastating). On positive images, negative wording moves \qwen{}'s judgment four to five times farther than positive wording; all six pairs agree, and the within-item contrast is $+1.148$. Run without an image, the same six pairs show no detectable difference, which argues against a large imbalance in sentence strength but cannot exclude a small one. A set of six unrelated events points the same way but does not survive resampling the events. In \gemma{}, where a text-trained valence probe is available, replacing downstream states at text positions restores $88$--$93\%$ of the context difference, and a direction estimated only from valenced text still moves the answer under conflict. Across four models the conclusion depends on the summary score and on how multi-token labels are scored: scoring only a label's first piece manufactures a null in one model and reverses the categorical ordering in another. Negative text can therefore exert disproportionate influence on image judgments, though the measured size depends on model and measurement. Evaluations should test what each cue says, not only which modality carries it.
\end{abstract}

\ifshort
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Interpretability tools are used to explain what a vision-language model is doing with its inputs. A tool that localizes an effect, however, localizes whatever the measurement puts in front of it, including an artifact. We report a case where three standard tools were applied to one phenomenon: two of the resulting conclusions held, and a third did not, for a reason that stayed invisible until the measurement changed.

The phenomenon is a cross-modal valence conflict. We pair EMOTIC photographs of people \citep{kosti2019} with one-sentence contexts that contradict them and ask whether negative wording pulls the emotion judgment farther than matched positive wording. It does. Holding the photograph and the described event fixed and flipping a single valence word, all six tested pairs agree, with a within-item contrast of $+1.148$ on positive images (\S\ref{sec:conflict}).

Onto that phenomenon we apply a frozen text-trained linear probe, activation patching, and a difference-of-means intervention. Two claims survive. The probe transfers across modalities, and patching localizes states sufficient to restore each cue's effect: downstream text positions restore $88$--$93\%$ of the context difference in \gemma{}, while the image's own valence is recovered from image positions early in the network and from text positions late (\S\ref{sec:mechanism}).

Two failures qualify them, and they are as much the contribution as the successes. \textbf{First, localization is readout-dependent.} In the one layer band where both of our readouts are valid, they give non-overlapping intervals in opposite directions about which token group dominates --- from the same model, the same run, and the same intervention. Nothing distinguishes them except where the readout is taken. \textbf{Second, the cross-model conclusion was an artifact of a scoring choice.} Evaluating only the first token of a multi-token emotion label manufactured a null in one model and reversed the categorical ordering in another. Correcting it dissolved an apparent architectural boundary that earlier analyses of this same data had reported (\S\ref{sec:models}).

We therefore present this as a study of when localization evidence does and does not license a conclusion, not as a claim about vision-language architecture. We do not claim to discover that text can override vision \citep{deng2025blindfaith, contextvqa2026}, nor the first mechanistic study of multimodal conflict \citep{seeingoverrides2026}. Appendix~\ref{app:related} positions the work against prior conflict, emotion, and mechanistic-interpretability literature. The behavioral pattern is consistent with negativity dominance in humans \citep{rozin2001, baumeister2001}; we treat it as an analogue, not the same construct.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Show a vision-language model a smiling face alongside the sentence \emph{``This was taken at her best friend's funeral,''} and it must decide which signal to follow. Prior work shows that this choice varies with the task, model, prompt, relevance of the text, and relation between the two modalities \citep{deng2025blindfaith, mixedsignals2025, contextvqa2026, signpost2026}. One question remains open: under otherwise matched conditions, does positive text pull the model as strongly as negative text? The answer has practical weight. Systems that read affect from images of people, such as content moderation queues that triage distressing material, assistive agents that respond to a user's mood, and intake tools that summarize a scene, take that text from the user or from surrounding context, not from a curated benchmark. If negative phrasing moves those judgments further than positive phrasing does, the error is directional, and it falls hardest on people whom the accompanying text already describes badly.

Human affective science suggests it should not. Negativity dominance (the finding that mixtures of negative and positive cues are judged more negatively than their parts predict) is among the most replicated results in psychology \citep{rozin2001, baumeister2001}, and context sentences reshape how people read facial emotion \citep{aviezer2008, aviezer2012}. Whether VLMs show an analogous asymmetry, in which models, and through what computation, remains an open question.

We pair emotionally extreme EMOTIC photographs \citep{kosti2019} with positive, negative, and neutral one-sentence contexts and score both conflict directions against each image's neutral-context baseline. In the most controlled condition we hold the photograph and event fixed and change one valence word. On positive images, all six pairs show the same asymmetry: negative wording moves \qwen{} four to five times farther than positive wording (within-item contrast $+1.148$, photo-clustered 95\% CI $[+0.94,+1.34]$). With no image, the pairs show no detectable mean difference, arguing against a large wording imbalance but not excluding a small one.

We then ask where the model keeps each cue's signal. In \gemma{}, a frozen text-trained valence probe \citep{tak2025} reads image valence, downstream text-position states restore $88$--$93\%$ of the context difference, and a direction estimated from text still moves the answer under conflict. Across four models, however, effect size and sometimes sign depend on the summary score and complete-label scoring. We therefore claim a controlled asymmetry in the tested setup, not a universal architectural law.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
  \item A controlled behavioral test of valence asymmetry in VLMs: holding the photograph and event fixed and flipping one valence word, negative context moves \qwen{}'s image-grounded judgment four to five times farther than positive context, with all six tested pairs in agreement, while the same sentence pairs without an image show no detectable difference.
  \item A mechanistic account in \gemma{} of where each cue's effect lives: a frozen text-trained valence probe transfers to image-conditioned states, and activation patching, which copies activations from one run into another to see which states carry an effect, shows downstream text positions are sufficient to restore most of the context-driven difference and an increasing share of the image-driven difference with depth.
  \item A measurement caution from testing four models and three readouts: effect size and sometimes sign depend on scoring choices, most sharply when a multi-token emotion label is scored by its first token instead of in full, so we report a controlled asymmetry in the tested setup, not a universal architectural claim.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Conflict between an image and text.}
Several lines of work build mismatched image-text pairs and ask which modality wins: VLMs over-trust corrupted text, the preferred modality shifts with task and model, and persuasive text can override clear visual evidence \citep{deng2025blindfaith, mixedsignals2025, contextvqa2026, signpost2026, camel2025}. A parallel literature pits the image against knowledge held in the weights, where image tokens carry nearly all of the effect \citep{nooralahzadeh2026, lietzow2026, seeingoverrides2026, unraveling2024}. Our competing text sits in the prompt rather than the weights, and none of this work asks whether matched positive and negative text pull with equal force, which is what we test in both directions across four model designs.

\paragraph{Emotion, affect, and valence asymmetry.}
EMOTIC and related datasets are standard for VLM emotion recognition \citep{kosti2019, etesam2024, agarwal2026, mosear2025, emomm2026}, and \citet{veena2026} intervene on emotion circuits, though their cues agree. In text-only LLMs, \citet{tak2025} locate appraisal information and steer emotion judgments, the method we build on, and \citet{negbeforepos2026} report layer-wise differences between positive and negative valence. The hypothesis comes from human work on negativity dominance \citep{rozin2001, baumeister2001} and on situational effects in facial-affect perception \citep{aviezer2008, aviezer2012}.

\paragraph{Locating and moving the signal.}
Activation patching tests which internal states suffice to restore an output difference \citep{meng2022, patchingbestpractices, attributionpatching}, and in VLMs image information becomes readable at text positions in early-to-middle layers \citep{basu2024, whatsintheimage2025, narrowgate2024, towardsinterp2024, fcct2026}. The closest precedent for moving an intervention across modalities is \citet{luo2025}, who transfer \emph{task} vectors; we ask instead whether a valence direction estimated from text can move an image-conditioned judgment under disagreement. Because delimiter tokens pool information across a prompt \citep{xiao2024, activedormant2024, zhang2026anydepth} and difference-of-means steering can change outputs without revealing what computation changed \citep{steeringunreliability2025, steeringnonident2026}, we benchmark against equal-norm random directions and a different appraisal. Appendix~\ref{app:related} gives the full positioning, including \citet{nikankin2025} and \citet{conflictchallenges2025}.

\section{Experimental framework}
\label{sec:setup}


\paragraph{Task and stimuli.}
Each trial combines an EMOTIC photograph with a context and asks, \emph{``What single emotion is this person feeling?''}\ifshort\else\ (Figure~\ref{fig:method})\fi. We use 150 extreme-valence person annotations from 121 distinct test images. The \emph{varied set} contains six unrelated events per valence and two neutral contexts; the \emph{matched set} contains six same-event pairs differing in one valence word. We compute every effect per image against the mean neutral-context response, holding sentence presence fixed (Appendix~\ref{app:nocontext} explains why a no-context prompt is not the baseline). Appendix~\ref{app:contexts} gives the complete stimuli and prompt. One image contributes people to both polarity groups; excluding it changes the headline contrast by less than $0.01$.

\paragraph{Models.}
We evaluate \qwen{}, \gemma{}, \llava{}, and \llavanext{} through their reference HuggingFace implementations in bf16. We treat \qwen{} as the main behavioral-evaluation model because image and text receive comparable regression weight on this task, and \gemma{} as the model for mechanism analysis because we fit the text-trained probe on its states. We score forward-pass probabilities, not generated answers. Appendix~\ref{app:compute} reports compute and \S\ref{sec:limitations} describes the superseded wrapper runs.

\paragraph{Readouts.}
We sum complete-label log probabilities for 13 fixed emotion words and normalize within that list.\ifshort\else\ This matters because several LLaVA labels split into multiple tokens; first-token scoring changes two qualitative conclusions.\fi\ We read the answer three ways, because no single summary is neutral across models: \ifshort(i) a neutral-baseline-corrected categorical override gap, (ii) bounded valence $P(\text{positive})-P(\text{negative})$, and (iii) unbounded positive-versus-negative log-odds.\else the \emph{override gap}, how often the top-scoring label flips valence category, corrected against that image's neutral-context error rate; \emph{bounded valence}, the probability mass difference $P(\text{positive})-P(\text{negative})$, which saturates near the ends of $[-1,1]$; and an \emph{unbounded log-odds} margin, $\log P(\text{positive})/P(\text{negative})$, which has no ceiling and keeps resolving differences the bounded score compresses.\fi\ The \emph{mirror contrast} compares the two conflict directions; the \emph{within-item contrast} additionally holds the event fixed. Appendix~\ref{app:contexts} gives the label and scoring details, including surface-form and length sensitivities \citep{holtzman2021, brown2020}.

\paragraph{Internal readout.}
Following \citet{tak2025}, we train a ridge probe for pleasantness on crowd-enVENT text states \citep{troiano2023}, select Gemma layer 18 on validation data, freeze it, and apply it to images. Shuffled-label and random-direction controls accompany the readout. For steering, we add the high-minus-low pleasantness mean direction estimated from text \citep{turner2023, rimsky2024}; this intervention is distinct from the probe direction.

The probe is fit on \gemma{} text states and its site selected at layer 18 on validation ($r^2=0.641$, a selection statistic, not a held-out estimate). \section{The phenomenon under study}
\label{sec:conflict}
\label{sec:integration}
\label{sec:asymmetry}
\label{sec:minimal}

Both cues measurably affect the answer: standardized regression weights on image valence and context valence are positive in all four models. \qwen{} weights them most comparably ($|\betatxt|/|\betaimg|=1.14$; the others range from $0.44$ to $0.68$), so an asymmetry it shows cannot be explained by it under-using the image. It is our behavioral model for that reason.

On the varied set of six unrelated events per valence, a negative context moves positive-image valence by $-1.156$ while a positive context moves negative-image valence by $+0.762$; the per-image mirror contrast is $+0.409$ (photo-clustered 95\% CI $[+0.23,+0.59]$). That interval treats the six sentences as fixed. Resampling photographs and sentences jointly --- the test that licenses a claim about negative \emph{language} and not just these six sentences \citep{clark1973, westfall2014} --- widens it to $[-0.16,+0.91]$, which includes zero.

The matched set removes the between-event variance that causes this. Six pairs keep the event and token count fixed and change one valence word (won$\leftrightarrow$lost, wonderful$\leftrightarrow$devastating). Its mirror contrast is $+0.496$ with a crossed interval excluding zero ($[+0.11,+0.83]$). On positive images the strict within-item contrast, same photograph, same event, one word changed, is $+1.148$ (photo-clustered $[+0.943,+1.344]$, with the six pairs held fixed; Wilcoxon $p<0.001$ over the 62 positive-group photograph averages), and all six pairs agree with ratios from $4.3$ to $5.5$. Run without an image, the same pairs show no detectable mean difference ($+0.33$ log-odds, $p=0.69$), which argues against a large imbalance in sentence strength but cannot exclude a small one. Appendix~\ref{app:behavior} reports the full factorial matrix, every pair, and the baseline-corrected override gaps.

\section{What the interventions localize}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\label{sec:patching}
\label{sec:crosspatch}
\label{sec:arbitration}

\paragraph{Where the sentence's effect can be restored.}
Projecting \gemma{}'s last-token state onto the frozen probe at every layer, the positive-versus-negative context gap clears the early-layer noise around layer 12 of 34 (Appendix~\ref{app:layerwise}), so we intervene just downstream, over layers 13--17. Holding the image fixed, we replace the negative-context activations of one aligned token group at a time with those from the positive-context run and measure how far the readout moves. The scored token is excluded from every group.

\input{tables/patching}

Replacing all aligned downstream text states restores $88$--$93\%$ of the context difference (Table~\ref{tab:patching}). No single group is sufficient, and the two sentence pairs disagree about how the recovery divides: on pair 1 the question tokens and the tokens before the model's answer restore indistinguishable shares, while on pair 2 the question-token estimate is larger and the two individual estimates sum to more than the joint recovery. We therefore report only the shared claim and treat patching effects as non-additive. The lower rows are alignment checks: those positions precede the context and the image is held fixed, so under causal masking the patch copies a value onto itself and the zeros are arithmetic, not measured.

\paragraph{Where the image's effect can be restored.}
Holding the context fixed and neutral and swapping the image instead, the image-driven difference is recovered almost entirely from image positions early ($100.1\%$ $[99.6,100.5]$ at layers 0--12) and barely at all from text positions ($1.7\%$ $[-0.6,4.6]$). Late in the network the ordering reverses: image positions recover $31.1\%$ $[24.1,38.4]$ and text positions $63.4\%$ $[55.1,71.5]$. The visual difference therefore becomes \emph{additionally} readable from text positions with depth rather than leaving the image states \citep{basu2024, whatsintheimage2025}.

\paragraph{The localization is readout-dependent.}
In the mid band, layers 13--17, both of our readouts are valid and they disagree about which group dominates. The layer-18 probe gives image $74.9\%$ $[71.3,78.9]$ against text $87.4\%$ $[85.9,88.8]$; behavioral valence gives image $96.0\%$ $[92.1,99.6]$ against text $74.1\%$ $[66.5,81.6]$. Both orderings rest on non-overlapping intervals, in opposite directions, from the same model and the same intervention. The only difference is where the readout is taken --- inside layer 18 against after sixteen further layers of mixing. A paper reporting either number alone would state a confident and opposite localization result. We report both and draw no conclusion about mid-band ordering.

\paragraph{The intervention still works under conflict.}
Adding the text-derived pleasantness direction at layer 18 moves behavioral valence with slope $+0.335$ across 150 conflict trials. This shows the intervention remains effective while an image contradicts the sentence; it does not show that the competition between the cues changed, since a general output-valence direction could move the score without altering either cue's weight.

Together these interventions locate states \emph{sufficient} to restore each cue's effect. They do not establish necessity, and they do not explain why negative context moves behavior farther --- a symmetric model would produce the same pattern of information flow.

\subsection{Mechanism: which internal states restore each cue?}
\label{sec:mechanism}

Two replacement experiments follow, both on \gemma{}, because the text-trained probe was fit on its states. \gemma{} is not the model carrying the behavioral contrast of \S\ref{sec:minimal}, so what follows locates where a context effect lives in \gemma{}; it is not a mechanism for the \qwen{} asymmetry. Both experiments point to the same place: downstream text positions are where the sentence's effect and the image's own valence become jointly readable.

\subsubsection{Which token positions carry the sentence's effect?}
\label{sec:patching}

Before asking \emph{where} the context signal travels, we first located \emph{when} it appears. We project the last-token state onto the frozen probe direction at every layer; the gap between positive- and negative-context runs first clears the noise around layer 12 of 34, where it is already large and consistent (Appendix~\ref{app:layerwise}). We therefore intervene just downstream of that, over layers 13--17.

Which token positions carry the context's valence signal? We hold the image constant and compare a positive-context run with a negative-context run. For one group of token positions at a time, we replace the negative-context activations over layers 13--17 with the corresponding activations from the positive-context run, then measure how much the readout moves toward the positive-context value. We exclude the final scored token from every patched group, so the experiment cannot recover the answer by directly replacing that state. The result repeats across two context pairs (Table~\ref{tab:patching}); Appendix~\ref{app:patching} gives the full method.

Replacing all aligned downstream text states restores $88$--$93\%$ of the context difference. Within those positions, no single group is sufficient, but the two sentence pairs differ. On pair 1 the question tokens and the tokens marking the start of the model's answer restore statistically indistinguishable shares ($49.1\%$ $[45.0, 53.4]$ vs.\ $45.2\%$ $[43.3, 47.2]$), and their sum lies inside the interval for replacing all aligned text at once. On pair 2 the question-token estimate is larger ($54.8\%$ $[52.1, 58.0]$ vs.\ $38.2\%$ $[35.6, 40.5]$), and the individual estimates sum to more than the joint recovery. We therefore make only the shared claim: neither group alone restores the full difference, and patching effects should not be interpreted as additive causal shares.

The lower rows are alignment checks, not evidence about where visual information lives; the caption and Appendix~\ref{app:patching} explain why they are zero by construction, and record three limits on what this experiment establishes.

\subsubsection{Where does the image's own valence live?}
\label{sec:crosspatch}

We next ask where the model keeps the \emph{image's own} valence. We hold the context fixed and neutral, then compare a positive-image run with a negative-image run. Because the text inputs are identical, every text position matches exactly. Table~\ref{tab:crosspatch} reports how much of the image-driven difference we recover when we patch activations over three layer ranges. Behavioral valence is the main readout; the layer-18 probe agrees in the ranges where it is valid.

Early in the network, image positions alone recover almost the entire image-driven difference ($100.1\%$ $[99.6, 100.5]$), while text positions recover essentially none of it ($1.7\%$ $[-0.6, 4.6]$). Late in the network the ordering reverses: image-position recovery falls to $31.1\%$ $[24.1, 38.4]$ while text-position recovery rises to $63.4\%$ $[55.1, 71.5]$. We read this as the visual difference becoming \emph{additionally} recoverable from text positions with depth, rather than migrating out of the image positions: we still recover a third of it there at layers 18--28. This agrees with prior evidence of image-to-text transfer \citep{basu2024, whatsintheimage2025}.

In the mid band, where both readouts are valid, they disagree about which group dominates: the layer-18 probe gives image $74.9\%$ $[71.3, 78.9]$ vs.\ text $87.4\%$ $[85.9, 88.8]$, while behavioral valence gives image $96.0\%$ $[92.1, 99.6]$ vs.\ text $74.1\%$ $[66.5, 81.6]$. Non-overlapping intervals support both orderings, in opposite directions. Both come from the same model and inference stack, so the disagreement reflects \emph{where} we take the readout (at layer 18 versus after sixteen further layers of mixing), not a measurement error. We report both and draw no conclusion about mid-band ordering.

The same broad set of downstream text positions can therefore restore differences caused by either the context or the image. This identifies a plausible meeting point for the two cues, not the computation that resolves their competition. It also does not show whether visual information continues to move into text positions while negative context is winning. In a confirmatory run with fixed negative context, the image-driven valence gap shrinks from $0.87$ to $0.08$. That small denominator makes late-layer recovery unstable, so we could not answer the question. Mid-layer image-position recovery is $85\%$ (CI $[51,150]$), but the wide interval prevents a stronger conclusion.

A text-derived valence direction added at layer 18 also remains effective while an image contradicts the sentence; Appendix~\ref{sec:arbitration} reports that sweep.

\section{Where the measurement misleads}
\label{sec:models}

The same phenomenon, measured on four models, produced an architectural story that did not survive a change to the scoring rule.

\paragraph{A scoring choice manufactured a null and reversed an ordering.}
Emotion words are not all one token. In \qwen{} and \gemma{} every label in our fixed 13-word list is a single token, but both LLaVA models split several --- \emph{boredom} and \emph{disgust} take three each. The original analysis scored only the first content token, which compares the beginning of one word against the whole of another. Scoring each complete label instead leaves every trial and every stimulus unchanged and overturns the comparison: \llava{} moves from the analysis's only null to its strongest categorical result ($+63.9\%$), and \llavanext{}'s corrected categorical gap reverses to $-11.2\%$. An apparent boundary between connector architectures rested entirely on the discarded token.

\input{tables/models}

\paragraph{Nor does averaging fix it.}
Length-normalizing the token scores is the standard alternative \citep{brown2020}, and here it fails worse. Our options are single words, so once the first token commits to an unusual word the remainder is nearly certain, and the mean rewards long labels almost mechanically: it correlates with label length at $r=0.93$ in \llava{} and puts $2{,}246$ of $2{,}250$ trials on one of the two three-token words. Summing leaves a much smaller bias, $r=-0.23$, which runs \emph{against} the reported effect. There is no neutral choice here, only a defensible one that must be stated.

\paragraph{The readouts disagree, and no boundary replaces the one that fell.}
On the varied set, \llava{} clears all three crossed tests, \gemma{} two, \llavanext{} one, and \qwen{} none; its support instead comes from the matched set of \S\ref{sec:conflict}. The three readouts answer related but different questions, so we report the disagreement instead of picking the favorable measure per model. Connector family and image-token count do not organize what remains: the two LLaVA models share a connector family yet diverge sharply on the categorical readout, and a \qwen{} resolution sweep is flat across image-token budgets while the four cross-model results are non-monotonic. Because tokenizer, backbone, vision tower and connector vary together, we report the variation as unexplained instead of fitting a story to it.

\paragraph{What this costs the mechanism claims.}
The localization results of \S\ref{sec:mechanism} are detailed for \gemma{} alone, because the probe was fit on its states. Coarser behavioral-readout replacement runs on \qwen{} and \llava{} restore $62\%$ and $66\%$ of the context difference from all text positions jointly, against $82\%$ for \gemma{} on the same context pair and readout; image positions restore none in any of the three. Position groups are non-additive in every model and we assign them no causal shares. We have not examined \llavanext{} internally.

\subsection{How far the result travels: models, readouts, and label scoring}
\label{sec:models}

Is the asymmetry a property of VLMs in general, or of particular designs? We run the same conflict experiment on four ways of connecting vision to language. \qwen{} uses a native vision transformer accepting varying resolutions. \gemma{} pools its SigLIP output to 256 image tokens. \llava{} passes unpooled patches from a frozen CLIP tower through a linear projection. \llavanext{} uses that same linear-projector design at a far larger token budget (2{,}147 image tokens).

Because each model uses the bounded valence scale differently, no single readout is neutral (Appendix~\ref{app:headroom}). We therefore report three: the categorical \emph{override rate} (\S\ref{sec:setup}), the bounded \emph{mirror contrast}, and an \emph{unbounded log-odds margin}, $\log P(\text{pos})/P(\text{neg})$, which removes the scale's bounds entirely. Each carries both interval types defined above, and the crossed one, which treats the sentences as a sample rather than as fixed \citep{clark1973, westfall2014}, is the stricter test and the one that licenses claims about negative language in general (Table~\ref{tab:models}).

Three results follow.

\textbf{Complete-label scoring changes both LLaVA conclusions.} The original analysis used the first content token for every label. That is complete for Qwen and Gemma here, but not for LLaVA: every LLaVA label begins with a shared prefix token, and several labels contain additional content tokens. Scoring the whole label leaves the trial metadata unchanged but overturns the comparison. \llava{} moves from a null to a strong positive result on all three readouts (Figure~\ref{fig:conflict}). \llavanext{} moves in the other direction: its corrected categorical gap is $-11.2\%$, its bounded mirror contrast falls to $+0.125$, and only its unbounded margin retains a crossed interval above zero. The complete-label score is primary because it evaluates the answer the prompt asks for, not a prefix of it.

The text-only control does not explain either result. Without an image, \llava{}'s negative and positive sentences have similar strength (raw unbounded ratio $0.89$). For \llavanext{}, the negative sentences are markedly \emph{weaker} (ratio $0.39$), yet its image-conditioned unbounded contrast is positive. The LLaVA findings therefore are not inherited from stronger negative sentences. A content-token mean gives very different categorical results, but it strongly favors longer labels, so we retain it only as a sensitivity check (Appendix~\ref{app:contexts}).

\textbf{The readouts disagree, and no clean model boundary remains.} On the varied set, \llava{} clears all three crossed tests, \gemma{} clears the bounded and unbounded tests, \llavanext{} clears only the unbounded test, and \qwen{} clears none. \qwen{}'s support under sentence resampling comes from the matched set of \S\ref{sec:minimal}, where every word pair points the same way. The categorical and graded measures are answering related but different questions: whether the top label crosses a valence category, how probability mass moves on a bounded scale, and how the positive-to-negative odds move without bounds. We therefore report all three instead of choosing the favorable measure for each model. The list is also unbalanced, four positive words against seven negative, giving the argmax more ways to land negative. Re-scoring on all $35$ balanced four-negative subsets leaves the graded result intact (the within-item contrast excludes zero in $35/35$, median $+1.144$) but not the categorical one ($20/35$, reversing to $-0.714$ once \emph{anger} and \emph{sadness} are dropped), a further reason we let the graded contrast carry the claim.

The neutral correction still matters, especially for \qwen{} and \gemma{}. \qwen{} starts with more negative errors on positive images, while \gemma{} starts with more positive errors on negative images; subtracting those image-specific neutral rates moves \qwen{} down and \gemma{} up. We flag that we applied this categorical correction after seeing the uncorrected results. It follows the neutral-baseline rule already used for the graded readouts, but its effect should not be mistaken for a model property.

\textbf{Two simple architectural explanations do not organize the results.} Both LLaVA models use a frozen CLIP tower and linear projector, and both have positive unbounded contrasts under complete-label scoring. They nevertheless differ sharply on the categorical readout, while also changing language backbone and image-token count. Token count is no simpler: within \qwen{}, a resolution sweep is flat from 128 to 262 image tokens with image discriminability held at AUC $0.983$--$0.986$, while the four cross-model results are non-monotonic. Because tokenizer, backbone, vision tower and connector vary together, we report the architectural variation as unexplained. We have not run the most direct connector control, \texttt{llava-v1.6-vicuna-7b} (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Mechanism replication.} Repeating the replacement experiment on \qwen{} and \llava{} restores $62.3\%$ and $66.0\%$ of the context difference from all text positions jointly, against $81.6\%$ for \gemma{} on the same context pair and readout; image positions are an alignment check, zero by causal order (Table~\ref{tab:mechmodels}). Both use broader layer bands than \gemma{}; no model's position groups are additive, so we assign no causal shares. We ran none on \llavanext{}.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{The phenomenon.} The matched result uses six pairs in one sentence frame (``This photo was taken \ldots''). It excludes a large text-only strength imbalance but not a small one, and it establishes nothing about other wording. Crossed intervals are correspondingly wide and all runs use a single seed. EMOTIC annotates one person while we present the whole unmarked image; the single photograph contributing people to both valence groups moves the matched-set mirror contrast from $+0.496$ to $+0.505$.

\textbf{The interventions.} Patching establishes sufficiency, not necessity, and does not explain the asymmetry. The same-image zeros at positions preceding the context are forced by causal masking, not measured. We ran no matched non-affective control, so we cannot tell whether the role of the pre-answer tokens is specific to valence or a general property of where prompt information gathers. The varied-set sentences differ in token length, placing aligned groups at different absolute positions; a rerun on the token-matched pairs removes that confound. Mechanism analysis is detailed for \gemma{} only.

\textbf{The measurement.} Complete-label scoring repairs the first-token failure we demonstrate, but the fixed 13-label list remains vulnerable to unoffered synonyms \citep{holtzman2021}, and we cannot rule out further readout choices with comparable leverage. Reported image-conditioned results use reference HuggingFace implementations; we withdraw a prompt-rephrasing sweep and a within-family scale comparison produced through a wrapper that altered the multimodal forward pass, so question phrasing remains untested. Emotion inference from photographs is contested and we use it only as a diagnostic instrument, never as evidence that such judgments are valid; Appendix~\ref{app:impacts} gives the responsible-use discussion in full.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Three standard interpretability tools, applied to one controlled cross-modal conflict, produced two conclusions that held and one that did not. The probe transferred across modalities and patching localized states sufficient to restore each cue's effect; those results stand. The cross-model generalization did not, because scoring only the first token of a split emotion word manufactured a null in one model and reversed another, and no amount of care in the patching would have exposed it --- the error lived in the readout, upstream of every intervention. The mid-band disagreement makes the same point inside a single model. Localization evidence is only as good as the readout it is taken through, and the readout is what needed checking first.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Where the cues meet.}
Repeating the intervention separately for neutral-to-positive and neutral-to-negative changes would test whether the asymmetry appears at the meeting point identified in \S\ref{sec:crosspatch} or is already present upstream.

\paragraph{Why do the models differ?}
We do not know. The complete-label rerun removes the apparent \llava{} null and with it the cleanest architectural contrast. The two LLaVA models share a connector family but differ in backbone, token budget and the size of each readout. Image-token count is non-monotonic across models and flat in the within-\qwen{} sweep. The remaining variation may reflect any of these model differences, or how their probability distributions interact with the readout. The present comparison does not isolate a cause.

\paragraph{Conflicts with stored model knowledge.}
In conflicts between an image and knowledge stored in the model's weights, prior work finds that image tokens carry most of the causal effect \citep{nooralahzadeh2026, lietzow2026}. In our conflicts, the competing text is part of the prompt, and its effect travels through text-token states. The readout can even recover the image signal from text positions. Where a conflict is resolved therefore depends on the type of conflict, the layer, and the model design.

\paragraph{Implications.}
On the affect-sensitive task studied here, a single negative sentence overrides \qwen{}'s visual judgment on $57\%$ of conflict trials against $35\%$ in the mirror direction, once we subtract each image's neutral-sentence baseline. Uncorrected, the same comparison reads $76\%$ against $37\%$. The LLaVA reruns add a second caution: even the sign of the categorical gap can depend on whether we score a split label as a complete word. We therefore caution against treating any single figure as \emph{the} magnitude of negativity dominance in VLMs. In the tested setup, the result depends on the text's valence, the model, and the decision rule. In \gemma{}, downstream text positions are a useful target for further study because replacing them restores most of the context difference and intervening there changes the output. Generalizing this result to other models requires replication.

\paragraph{What this means for deployed systems.}
A pipeline that reads affect from an image is rarely given the image alone; it also gets whatever text arrives with it, a caption, a report, or a user's message. The rates above mean the two inputs are not weighed evenly, and that the text channel supplies a directional error a symmetric evaluation will not surface. We read this as a faithfulness problem, since the visual evidence does not change when the sentence does. Two things follow. An evaluation reporting only accuracy, or only which modality tends to win, misses the asymmetry entirely; it appears only when both conflict directions are measured against a neutral baseline on the same images. And the states we localize give somewhere to look: in \gemma{} the text positions carrying the sentence's effect also carry a growing share of the image's own valence with depth, making them a candidate monitoring site, though we tested them as an analysis tool, not a mitigation.

\paragraph{Broader impacts and responsible use.}
Inferring emotion from photographs of people is a contested application, and our use of it is diagnostic: we treat the model's emotion judgment as an instrument for studying how it combines modalities, not as evidence that such judgments are valid. We do not intend any of this as support for deploying affect inference on images of people. Appendix~\ref{app:impacts} sets out three risks that follow from the results specifically, and our data-release terms.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\label{sec:limitations}
The patching experiments show which states are sufficient to restore each cue's effect, not why negative context produces the larger one, and the mechanism analysis is detailed only for \gemma{}; the \qwen{} and \llava{} replications are coarser, and we ran none on \llavanext{}. The behavioral result rests on one sentence frame (``This photo was taken \ldots''), one dataset, and one task (person-emotion labeling); crossed intervals that resample both photographs and sentences are wide on the varied set and clear zero mainly through the matched set (\S\ref{sec:minimal}), so generalization to other context-sentence styles is untested. Complete-label scoring corrects the first-token failure documented above, but the fixed 13-label list remains vulnerable to unoffered synonyms \citep{holtzman2021}, and two further experiments, a rephrasing sweep and a within-family scale comparison, used a wrapper path that altered the multimodal forward pass, so we withdraw their results and question phrasing remains untested. All runs use a single seed, and our claims concern controlled image-context emotion judgments in the tested models, not a universal architectural pattern.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

On positive images, flipping a single valence word with the photograph and event held fixed moves \qwen{}'s emotion judgment four to five times farther when the word is negative, and all six pairs agree. That matched comparison carries the claim: on the varied set the estimate does not survive resampling the events. In \gemma{}, downstream text positions restore $88$--$93\%$ of the context difference and a text-derived direction still moves the output under conflict. Across four models no architectural boundary emerges, and first-piece scoring of a split emotion word manufactures a null in one and reverses another's ordering: a controlled valence asymmetry in the tested setup, not a universal law.

\section{Extended related work}
\label{app:related}

The full positioning, with the citations each claim rests on, is given here.

\paragraph{Modality conflict in VLMs.}
Several lines of work create mismatched image-text pairs and ask which modality the model follows. \citet{deng2025blindfaith} show that VLMs disproportionately trust text when it has been corrupted, while \citet{mixedsignals2025} find that the preferred modality varies with task complexity and model. Contradictory persuasive text can override clear visual evidence across 11 VLMs \citep{contextvqa2026}, including in geolocation \citep{signpost2026}. Misleading instructions can also become \emph{more} influential when an image is present \citep{camel2025}. These studies show that image-text conflict is real and can matter for safety. They examine factors such as relevance, task difficulty, and the relation between the cues, but do not isolate whether matched positive and negative text exert different pull. We test that question in both conflict directions and across model designs.

\paragraph{Different kinds of conflict.}
A complementary line of work studies conflicts between visual input and knowledge stored in the model's weights. \citet{nooralahzadeh2026} find that image tokens carry nearly all of the effect when a model must override such prior knowledge; \citet{lietzow2026} identify a small set of late attention heads involved in that override; \citet{seeingoverrides2026} steer the resulting decisions; and \citet{unraveling2024} both detect such conflicts and mitigate them by suppressing logits from the less confident modality. Our setting differs because the competing text appears directly in the prompt. In \gemma{}, even the image's decision-relevant signal becomes recoverable from text positions over depth. The location of conflict resolution may therefore depend on the kind of conflict, the network depth, and the model design.

\paragraph{Emotion and affect in multimodal models.}
EMOTIC and related datasets are widely used to test VLM emotion recognition \citep{kosti2019, etesam2024, agarwal2026}. Other benchmarks study emotion conflicts between audio and vision \citep{mosear2025, emomm2026}. VEENA \citep{veena2026} identifies and intervenes on emotion-related circuits in large VLMs, but its cues agree instead of conflicting and does not test valence asymmetry. In text-only LLMs, \citet{tak2025} locate appraisal-related information and steer emotion judgments, providing the method we build on. \citet{negbeforepos2026} report that positive and negative valence are processed differently across layers. We extend that question to behavioral conflicts between images and text.

\paragraph{Mechanistic interpretability of VLMs.}
Activation patching and causal tracing are established tools for testing which internal states are sufficient to restore an output difference \citep{meng2022, patchingbestpractices, attributionpatching}. Prior VLM studies show that image information becomes available from text or query positions in early-to-middle layers \citep{basu2024, whatsintheimage2025, narrowgate2024, towardsinterp2024}, and that middle-layer attention at the last token helps combine the modalities \citep{fcct2026}. Our contribution is to compare the states affected by each cue during conflict, not to establish image-to-text transfer itself. \citet{luo2025} show cross-modal transfer for \emph{task vectors}: a vector estimated from examples in one modality can trigger the corresponding task in another. That is the closest prior evidence for transferring an intervention vector across modalities, but it is not a demonstration of a valence direction. We instead ask whether a direction estimated from pleasant and unpleasant text examples can move an image-conditioned judgment when image and text disagree. \citet{nikankin2025} find largely separate circuits for similar visual and textual tasks, which remains compatible with different upstream computations affecting the same downstream states. \citet{conflictchallenges2025} find a linearly decodable \emph{conflict} signal in intermediate layers and present correlational evidence that conflict detection and resolution are separable. Our layerwise probe instead reads the sentence's valence, not conflict detection, and our replacement experiments test which states can restore an output difference; we do not equate the two signals. Other work finds that delimiter and assistant-header tokens can collect information from across a prompt \citep{xiao2024, activedormant2024, zhang2026anydepth}. In \gemma{} the context difference is split between the question tokens and the tokens before the model's answer (\S\ref{sec:patching}), so our data do not single out the turn scaffold.

\paragraph{Human affective science.}
Negativity dominance \citep{rozin2001} and ``bad is stronger than good'' \citep{baumeister2001} are foundational asymmetries in human evaluation, and situational sentences modulate facial-affect perception \citep{aviezer2008, aviezer2012}. We use this literature as motivation; whether VLM valence asymmetries share anything computational with the human construct is an open question our operationalization (cross-modal override) does not settle.

\paragraph{Steering vectors.}
Difference-of-means steering can change model outputs, but that does not by itself reveal what computation changed \citep{steeringunreliability2025, steeringnonident2026}. We compare our valence direction with random directions of the same norm and with a direction for a different appraisal. We therefore claim only that steering changes the outcome under conflict, not that it changes how strongly the model weights each cue (\S\ref{sec:arbitration}).



\section{Broader impacts and responsible use}
\label{app:impacts}

Inferring emotion from photographs of people is a contested application, and our use of it is diagnostic: we treat the model's emotion judgment as an instrument for studying how it combines modalities, not as evidence that such judgments are valid. We do not intend any of this as support for deploying affect inference on images of people. Three risks follow from the results specifically. \textbf{First,} one accompanying sentence can move the judgment of a system that reads images for affect, so a pipeline that accepts user-supplied text alongside images inherits a directional source of error. \textbf{Second,} the same internal site we use for measurement can also serve for intervention: a direction fit on text alone shifts image-conditioned output under conflict (\S\ref{sec:arbitration}). We report it to support scrutiny and monitoring, not as a deployment technique. \textbf{Third,} the resulting error may not be evenly distributed. If negative framing moves judgments farther than positive framing does, people who are more often described in negative terms may absorb more of the error, and we did not measure variation across demographic groups. The photographs are of real people, used under EMOTIC's research access terms; we release index files and context sentences, never images.

\section{The valence readout and steering direction, in full}
\label{app:foundations}

\subsection{Training the probe on text, and steering with it}
Linear probes test whether information can be recovered from a representation by a simple linear map \citep{alain2016}; following the recommendation to evaluate them against control tasks \citep{hewitt2019}, we report shuffled-label baselines throughout. For each appraisal, we fit a ridge probe to standardized last-token activations. We select the layer and activation site on the validation set ($n_{\rm train} = 4{,}320$, $n_{\rm val} = 1{,}080$). All six appraisals peak near the middle of the network at the multi-head attention output (Table~\ref{tab:stagea}). For steering, we use $\beta \cdot \Delta\mu_a$ at layer 18, where $\Delta\mu_a$ is the mean activation for high-rated examples minus the mean for low-rated examples. This difference-of-means construction is standard in steering work \citep{marks2023, li2023iti, zou2023, arditi2024}; we adopt it unchanged. The probe direction itself does not steer at any tested magnitude, while the difference-of-means direction produces a clear effect (Table~\ref{tab:steertext}).

\begin{table}[h]
  \caption{Probe localization: validation $r^2$ per appraisal at the best attention-output layer vs.\ shuffled-label baselines. These are layer-selection results, not untouched test estimates.}

\subsection{Does the text-trained probe read images? Caption controls}

\subsection{Does the text-derived direction still move the answer under conflict?}
\label{sec:arbitration}

Finally, we test whether the text-derived direction of \S\ref{sec:foundations} can change a judgment during conflict. On 150 conflict trials, adding the pleasantness direction at layer 18 moves behavioral valence with a slope of $+0.335$ per unit of intervention strength. The earlier no-conflict run gives a similar descriptive slope, $+0.336$, but it is not a matched control and we did not test equivalence; the supported claim is that the intervention remains effective under conflict. Before the sweep, we verified that the direction's length matched the reference value at this internal site (relative error $0.14\%$), guarding against an intervention at the wrong layer output.

The frozen probe readout is flat under this intervention (slope $+0.000$). That is expected, and not a null result: the probe tap sits inside layer 18, upstream of the residual-stream injection at the same layer, so it is invariant to steering by construction. We report it to show the injection is acting where we claim.

This intervention changes the outcome, but it does not show that the competition between the cues changed: a general output-valence direction could shift the final score without changing either cue's weight. The natural next step is to test whether the intervention changes override rates or cue coefficients.

\subsection{Combining directions (supporting evidence)}
In a 30-image pilot, combinations of text-derived appraisal directions produce specific emotions predicted by appraisal theory. Neither component alone produces anger: unpleasantness alone favors fear, while other-responsibility with its valence component removed favors joy. Combining the two at matched norm makes anger the top emotion on $97\%$ of images. This matches the appraisal prediction \emph{anger = negative + other-caused}. The pilot is supporting evidence that the appraisal directions have meaningful structure; it is not required for the conflict claims.

\section{Context sentences, prompt, and label set}
\label{app:contexts}

\paragraph{Label set.}
The 13 allowed emotion labels are anger, boredom, disgust, fear, guilt, joy, pride, relief, sadness, shame, surprise, trust, and neutral. Behavioral valence is the total probability of \{joy, pride, relief, trust\} minus the total probability of \{anger, boredom, disgust, fear, guilt, sadness, shame\}. Surprise and neutral contribute to neither side. This partition matches the released code.

\paragraph{Scoring split labels.}
LLaVA tokenizes every label into a shared prefix token followed by one to three content tokens. We sum the conditional log probabilities of every token in the label; the shared prefix adds the same constant to all 13 scores and therefore does not affect their normalized probabilities. The legacy first-content-token score changes \llava{} from a strong asymmetry to a null and changes \llavanext{} from one crossed test clearing zero to all three. We also tested averaging the content-token scores, which might appear to correct for word length. It does not: the mean score remains strongly correlated with token count ($r=0.93$ for \llava{}, $0.91$ for \llavanext{}) and makes nearly every trial select a negative label. We therefore use the complete-label sum and disclose the other two rules only as sensitivity checks.

\paragraph{Varied set.}
Table~\ref{tab:contextbank} lists the varied set verbatim, with each sentence's strength when run with no image and its mean behavioral valence on the positive-image group, both for \qwen{}. The text-only column is in log-odds instead of bounded valence because \qwen{} pins the bounded score to $\pm 1$ with no image present (Appendix~\ref{app:contexts}, above), which would make every sentence look identical. One negative sentence is markedly weaker than the rest in isolation (n1, $-4.9$ against $-11$ to $-20$ for the others) and is also the only negative sentence that fails to move the judgment on positive images ($+0.65$ against a neutral baseline of $+0.63$). The negative sentences are therefore not uniformly extreme, and the effect does not depend on any single one of them. The two neutral sentences also differ from each other ($+0.91$ and $+0.34$), which is why we measure every effect in the paper against their mean instead of against one of them.

\paragraph{The matched set.}
Each of the six pairs keeps the sentence structure fixed and changes only the valence-bearing word. The two sentences in every pair have equal token counts under both the Qwen tokenizer used for the headline result and the Gemma tokenizer used during stimulus development. The readout position is therefore identical within each pair. The exact sentences appear below, with the positive member first; each begins ``This photo was taken \ldots'':

  \item[mp5] \ldots during a \{joyful $\mid$ heartbreaking\} moment.
\end{itemize}
\paragraph{Do the sentences differ in isolation?}
The asymmetry could be a property of the sentences instead of the conflict: if the negative members are simply the stronger stimuli, they would dominate whatever the image showed. We test this directly by running every sentence with no image at all.

On \qwen{} the bounded valence readout cannot answer the question. With no image the model reads even a neutral sentence as sadness ($-0.996$), so $87\%$ of matched-set rows sit on a scale bound and the ratio comes out at exactly $1.00$ by construction: a number produced by two saturated constants, not by the sentences. We therefore read this control on the unbounded log-odds margin (\S\ref{sec:models}), which has no bounds to pin against.

On that scale the six matched pairs show no detectable mean difference. The pooled ratio is $|\text{neg}|/|\text{pos}|=1.02$; per pair it ranges from $0.67$ to $1.22$ with four of six leaning negative, and the mean paired difference is $+0.33$ log-odds (Wilcoxon $p=0.69$, $n=6$ pairs). The varied set gives $0.94$. With an image present the mean paired difference is $+10.46$ log-odds, and all six pairs point in the same direction (Wilcoxon $p=0.031$). The contrast between these controls argues against a large text-only imbalance as the source of the image-conditioned result.

Three caveats. Six pairs give little power, so this bounds a gross imbalance without excluding a small one. The control is per-model. Under complete-label scoring, the varied-set raw log-odds ratios are $0.89$ for \llava{} and $0.39$ for \llavanext{}: in both cases the negative sentences are no stronger than the positives without an image. Their neutral baselines are positive ($+0.29$ and $+1.48$ log-odds), while \qwen{} and \gemma{} lean negative without an image. Because a neutral sentence is not a common zero point across models, we take stimulus-strength ratios here against zero instead of against the neutral sentence.

The earlier \gemma{} figures of $1.06$ (varied) and $1.25$ (matched) were measured on the superseded inference stack and on the bounded readout. They reproduce exactly from those files and we record them as provenance, but they do not bear on the numbers above.

\paragraph{Prompt.}
The semantic prompt is identical across models, but each uses its native chat template. The Gemma form is shown here; the context part is omitted in the no-context condition:

<start\_of\_turn>user\textbackslash n<start\_of\_image>Context: \{ctx\} What single emotion is this person feeling?<end\_of\_turn>\textbackslash n<start\_of\_turn>model\textbackslash n

\section{Where in depth the context signal appears}
\label{app:layerwise}

Where in the network do positive- and negative-context runs begin to differ? At each layer we project the last-token state onto the frozen probe direction, reading a fixed direction off every depth in the manner of the logit lens and its successors \citep{nostalgebraist2020, belrose2023}, as a diagnostic, not a re-fitted probe. We summarize the gap between the two context polarities as a paired effect size over images, $d(L)$, which removes the growth in residual-stream norm with depth. The run covers 60 positive-group images; $51$ of them yield a complete positive/negative pair at every layer and enter the paired statistic.

The separation first clears the pre-divergence noise floor at layer 12 of 34, and it arrives already large: $|d| = 3.9$ there, against a floor set at three times the median absolute separation over the first eight layers. It does not then amplify: $|d|$ is $2.0$ at the readout layer 18 and $3.0$ at layer 28, with no late peak. In \emph{raw} projection units the separation does grow by more than an order of magnitude over the same span ($-0.40$ at layer 12 to $-6.10$ at layer 28), but that growth tracks the residual-stream norm, not the consistency of the effect, which is why we report the scale-free form. We therefore locate the entry of the context signal in the mid band and make no claim that it strengthens with depth. This shows where the difference becomes legible to this probe; it does not show where the signal is created.

The context's effect on the final token need not be direct: the final token could read the context words itself, or the context could first reshape other positions that the final token then reads. The replacement experiment in \S\ref{sec:patching} tests which downstream positions are sufficient to restore the difference.

\section{Correcting for the scale's bounds}
\label{app:headroom}

The valence score is bounded in $[-1,1]$, so changes can be compressed near either end. The main analysis removes the bounds with the log-odds score of \S\ref{sec:setup}. As a secondary check, we also divide each shift by the room remaining in the direction it moves.

Under that correction the asymmetry persists in \qwen{} (drop $0.714$ vs.\ rise $0.394$ on the varied set; $0.924$ vs.\ $0.518$ on the matched set) and in complete-label \llava{} (drop $0.643$ vs.\ rise $0.152$). It is weaker in \llavanext{} (drop $0.339$ vs.\ rise $0.263$), matching the ordering of the complete-label graded scores. We prefer the log-odds score in the main text because dividing by remaining room is unstable when the denominator is small: \gemma{}'s positive-image baseline sits at $+0.905$, close enough to the ceiling that the ratio becomes meaningless. We do not report a head-room-corrected number for \gemma{} for that reason.

\section{The no-context baseline confound}
\label{app:nocontext}

All analyses exclude the no-context condition and measure every effect against the neutral-sentence baseline. The reason is primarily a design one: the no-context prompt differs from every experimental condition in a way that has nothing to do with valence, namely whether a sentence is present at all. Baselining against a neutral sentence holds that difference fixed so the manipulation is purely polarity.

The empirical difference between the two baselines is small in the models we can measure it on (Table~\ref{tab:nocontext}): adding a neutral sentence shifts positive-image valence by $+0.059$ in \qwen{} and by $-0.011$ in \gemma{}. We note this explicitly because an earlier version of this appendix reported a much larger shift ($+0.411$) together with a matching move in the frozen probe readout, and concluded that the effect was representational. Those measurements came from the superseded inference stack described in \S\ref{sec:limitations}; the probe column is not available on the current stack, and we withdraw the representational claim. The design argument above does not depend on it.

\section{Pilot vs.\ varied set}
\label{app:pilot}

A 40-image pilot used one context sentence of each valence. It suggested that the image dominated (ratio $0.15$--$0.22$) and that the valence asymmetry was very large. Both conclusions depended on one weak positive sentence, and neither survived moving to the full sentence set. \section{Patching methodology}
\label{app:patching}

\paragraph{Same-image design (\S\ref{sec:patching}).}
For each image, the positive-context run supplies activations and the negative-context run receives them. We overwrite \texttt{resid\_post} across layers 13--17 for one aligned token group at a time. At the layer-18 probe, recovery is $(\text{patched} - \text{negative})/(\text{positive} - \text{negative})$. The groups are the 256 image tokens, question tokens, the beginning-of-sequence token, user-turn prefix markers, and the assistant-turn suffix (\texttt{<end\_of\_turn>\textbackslash n<start\_of\_turn>model}). We exclude the final scoring token from every group. The first, third and fourth of these groups lie before the context, and the image is identical in the two runs, so their donor and recipient states coincide and their recovery is $0\%$ by construction, not by measurement; they serve as a check on the position alignment.

We use 60 images for each of two sentence pairs: championship/funeral and wonderful/devastating. Recovery uses the same ratio-of-sums estimator as the cross-image design. The 60 annotation rows cover 51 distinct photographs, so rows sharing an image are collapsed before estimating and the bootstrap resamples those 51 units with $2{,}000$ resamples; \texttt{src/experiments/patching\_intervals.py} recomputes every interval in Tables~\ref{tab:patching} and~\ref{tab:crosspatch} from the saved per-row outputs. Behavioral-valence recovery orders the token groups the same way as probe recovery but is noisier; all-text recovery is $82$--$94\%$. The varied-set sentences differ in token length, so matched groups occur at different absolute positions. A planned rerun with token-matched pairs removes this positional confound.

\paragraph{Three limits on the same-image result.}
\textbf{First,} this experiment locates the positive-versus-negative difference; it does not explain why negative context has the larger behavioral effect. A symmetric model could show the same pattern of information flow. \textbf{Second,} the tokens before the answer may generally collect information from the prompt. Without a matched non-affective control, we cannot tell whether their role is specific to valence. \textbf{Third,} the $7$--$12\%$ not recovered by all aligned text is simply the remainder under this intervention. It is not an estimate of the context tokens' causal share, because patching effects need not add linearly. The varied-set sentences also differ in token length, placing aligned groups at different absolute positions. A planned rerun on the token-matched pairs removes this last confound.

\paragraph{Cross-image design (\S\ref{sec:crosspatch}).}
The positive-image run supplies activations and the negative-image run receives them, while the context stays fixed. Their \texttt{input\_ids} are identical, so every text position matches exactly. We use 60 image pairs and patch three layer ranges: 0--12, 13--17, and 18--28. Recovery is a ratio of sums, $\sum(\text{patched} - \text{neg}) / \sum(\text{pos} - \text{neg})$, rather than a mean of per-row ratios, because an individual pair's denominator can sit near zero; its interval is a percentile bootstrap over the 60 pairs with $2{,}000$ resamples. Behavioral valence is the main recovery measure. The layer-18 probe provides a check where valid; because its activation is computed from layer 17, it cannot measure recovery caused by patching layers 18--28. Patching every position except the scoring token recovers $99.9\%$, $100.0\%$, and $89.9\%$ across the three ranges. Under negative context, the image-driven gap shrinks to $0.08$, making the late-range denominator nearly zero. We report those confidence intervals but do not interpret them.

\paragraph{Qwen and LLaVA ports.}
For \qwen{} and \llava{} we patch through the standard HuggingFace implementation directly and score recovery on behavioral valence, on the same 51 photographs and the same championship/funeral context pair, across a broad middle-to-late layer range (\qwen{} layers 13--33 of 36; \llava{} layers 11--29 of 32). These bands are wider than \gemma{}'s 13--17 window, so the two ports are comparable to each other but coarser than the \gemma{} analysis. In \qwen{}, question tokens recover $12.1\%$, the turn markers $6.2\%$, and all aligned text positions together $62.3\%$; in \llava{} the same groups recover $32.7\%$, $20.5\%$, and $66.0\%$. In both, the joint recovery is much larger than the sum of the two individual estimates, showing that these interventions are non-additive; we therefore do not interpret the individual values as causal shares. Image positions again recover none, and again that is forced by prompt order, not measured (\S\ref{sec:patching}).

\section{Compute}
\label{app:compute}

The conflict and intervention experiments use inference-only forward passes in bf16 on one A100-class GPU with 40 GB of memory. They use no gradients or answer generation; the separate caption control generated descriptions for 1{,}000 images. Each model's varied-set base run is 150 person-annotations $\times$ 15 conditions $=$ 2{,}250 forward passes, and the \qwen{} matched-pair run is another 2{,}250; we run four models, each with a 15-pass text-only control. The conflict intervention sweep is 150 conflict trials $\times$ 7 strengths $=$ 1{,}050 passes. Layerwise localization is 60 positive-group images $\times$ 14 contexts $=$ 840 passes, each cached at every layer in a single forward. Same-image replacement covers 60 images $\times$ 2 sentence pairs $\times$ (7 position groups $+$ 2 baselines); cross-image replacement covers 60 image pairs $\times$ 3 layer bands $\times$ (6 position groups $+$ 2 baselines). The \qwen{} resolution sweep repeats the base run at three image resolutions. Counts here cover the reported experiments; two withdrawn subsections consumed a further 13{,}500 and 4{,}500 passes. \todo{Add wall-clock totals per run from the session logs for camera-ready; the full project including preliminary and failed runs used more compute than the reported experiments.}
