\begin{abstract}
Vision-language models (VLMs) can follow either modality when image and text disagree, but prior work has largely treated the competing evidence as interchangeable content. We ask whether conflict resolution depends on the \emph{valence} of the competing information. Pairing emotionally extreme person images from EMOTIC with controlled one-sentence contexts and measuring both conflict directions against neutral-context baselines, we find a valence asymmetry in \gemma{}: negative context changes positive-image judgments more than positive context changes negative-image judgments (mirror contrast $+0.265$, 95\% CI $[+0.10, +0.43]$, a ${\sim}1.8\times$ magnitude ratio), and negative context overrides a positive image on $84\%$ of trials versus $21\%$ for the mirror (gap $+64\%$). The asymmetry is about valence, not event semantics: token-matched minimal pairs that flip only the valence word (won/lost, wonderful/devastating) reproduce the override gap ($+65\%$ vs.\ $+64\%$), and the context banks have similar text-only strength. The effect is architecture-dependent rather than universal: it replicates on \qwen{} (gap $+39\%$) and persists from 4B to 12B, but is absent on the strongly image-anchored \llava{} (gap $-13\%$), bounding the phenomenon to two of the three vision-language unification designs we test. Mechanistically, in \gemma{} the context-polarity signal becomes detectable mid-stack and is carried causally through downstream text-stream states, and cross-image patching shows the image's own valence is not confined to image tokens: it migrates into the same text-stream positions over depth ($80\% \to 9\%$ image-token recovery from early to late layers), so both cues compete within a shared readout channel in which negative language wins. A text-derived valence direction causally shifts the outcome of ongoing conflicts (slope $+0.215$). Our results make the valence of competing evidence, and the model's unification architecture, first-class variables in multimodal conflict resolution.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

When a vision-language model is shown a smiling face alongside the sentence \emph{``This was taken at her best friend's funeral,''} which signal wins? A growing literature establishes that VLMs can prefer textual or visual evidence when the modalities disagree, with the preference swinging by task, model, and prompt \citep{deng2025blindfaith, mixedsignals2025, contextvqa2026, signpost2026}. This work, however, largely treats the two competing signals as interchangeable content: the question asked is \emph{which modality} wins, not whether arbitration depends on \emph{what} the competing evidence says.

We ask a sharper question: does cross-modal conflict resolution depend on the valence of the competing information? Human affective science suggests it should. Negativity dominance, the principle that combinations of negative and positive cues are evaluated more negatively than their parts predict, is among the most replicated findings in psychology \citep{rozin2001, baumeister2001}, and situational sentences modulate the perception of facial affect in valence-dependent ways \citep{aviezer2008, aviezer2012}. Whether VLMs exhibit an analogous asymmetry, in which models, and through what computation, has not been established.

We study this with a controlled conflict paradigm. Person images from the extremes of the EMOTIC valence distribution \citep{kosti2019} are paired with banks of positive, negative, and neutral one-sentence contexts; both conflict directions are run; every effect is measured against the neutral-context baseline; and the model's judgment is read both behaviorally (closed-vocabulary valence and a calibration-free override rate) and internally (a frozen linear appraisal probe trained only on text \citep{tak2025}, applied unchanged under image conditioning).

Four findings emerge. \textbf{First, a behavioral asymmetry (\gemma{}).} Negative context moves positive-image judgments by $-0.598$ while the mirror move, positive context on negative images, is $+0.333$: a mirror contrast of $+0.265$ (95\% CI $[+0.095, +0.431]$, ${\sim}1.8\times$), with override rates of $84\%$ versus $21\%$ (gap $+64\%$, CI $[+55, +72]$). Within positive-image trials, where head-room is symmetric, negative and positive contexts differ $3.6\times$ in displacement from neutral. \textbf{Second, the asymmetry is valence-specific.} A bank of six token-matched minimal pairs, identical sentences up to a single valence-bearing word (won$\leftrightarrow$lost, wonderful$\leftrightarrow$devastating), reproduces the override gap ($+65\%$, CI $[+56, +73]$) and sharpens the mirror contrast ($+0.330$, CI $[+0.151, +0.501]$), ruling out event-semantics explanations; text-only, the banks have similar mean strength, and the asymmetry appears only when the image is present ($80\%$ of the negative-text effect survives a positive image versus $24\%$ of the positive-text effect). \textbf{Third, the effect is architecture-dependent.} It replicates on \qwen{} (gap $+39\%$) and persists from \gemma{} to \gemmabig{}, but is \emph{absent} on \llava{} (gap $-13\%$; head-room-normalized pulls exactly symmetric at $0.33/0.33$): the strongly image-anchored linear-projector design resists textual override entirely. \textbf{Fourth, a mechanism in \gemma{}.} The context-polarity signal becomes detectable mid-stack under a fixed probe lens and is carried causally through downstream text-stream states (the assistant-turn boundary recovers $57$--$65\%$ of the context-induced delta; image tokens ${\sim}0\%$, as causal ordering predicts). Critically, cross-image patching shows the image's own valence is not confined to the image tokens: it is recoverable only from image tokens in early layers ($80\%$) but migrates into the same text-stream positions by late layers ($9\%$ vs.\ $68\%$). Both cues therefore compete within a shared text-stream readout channel, and negative language wins the competition there. A text-derived valence direction injected into that channel causally shifts conflict outcomes (slope $+0.215$).

Our contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Phenomenon.} A controlled demonstration of valence-asymmetric cross-modal conflict resolution, with both conflict directions, neutral baselining, a full factorial analysis, head-room and floor controls, and correctly scoped headline statistics (\S\ref{sec:conflict}).
  \item \textbf{Valence specificity.} A token-matched minimal-pair control isolating the valence variable from event semantics, including a stricter within-item paired test reported honestly as positive but marginal (\S\ref{sec:minimal}).
  \item \textbf{Architecture dependence.} The effect holds on two of three vision-language unification designs (Gemma-3, Qwen3-VL) and is absent on the third (\llava{}), identifying image-anchoring as a boundary condition rather than assuming universality (\S\ref{sec:models}).
  \item \textbf{Mechanism.} Same-image patching localizes the context-polarity signal to downstream text-stream states; cross-image patching shows visual valence migrates into those same states over depth, yielding a shared-channel account of the competition, with the causal-ordering and generic-bottleneck caveats stated explicitly (\S\ref{sec:mechanism}).
\end{itemize}

Throughout, we scope carefully. We do not claim to discover that text can override vision \citep{deng2025blindfaith, contextvqa2026}, nor the first mechanistic study of multimodal conflict \citep{seeingoverrides2026}, nor the first study of multimodal emotion conflict \citep{mosear2025}. We use \emph{valence-asymmetric conflict resolution} as the primary technical description and treat the phenomenon as a VLM analogue consistent with negativity-dominance findings in humans, not as the same construct.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Modality conflict in VLMs.}
Several lines of work create mismatched image-text pairs and ask which modality the model follows. \citet{deng2025blindfaith} show VLMs disproportionately trust text under corrupted-text conflicts; \citet{mixedsignals2025} find the preferred modality swings with task complexity and model; \citet{contextvqa2026} report that contradictory persuasive text overrides clear visual evidence across 11 VLMs; \citet{signpost2026} extend this to geolocation; and \citet{camel2025} find misleading instructions become \emph{more} influential when an image is present. These studies establish generic text-vision arbitration as real and safety-relevant, but treat the competing content as largely interchangeable. We add the valence axis, with affectively controlled conflicts in both directions and unimodal strength controls, and we add an architecture axis: the same conflict battery yields opposite outcomes on \llava{} versus Gemma/Qwen.

\paragraph{A conflict-type taxonomy.}
A complementary mechanistic literature studies conflicts between visual input and the model's \emph{internal parametric knowledge}: \citet{nooralahzadeh2026} find image tokens carry nearly all causal impact when a model must override its prior, and \citet{lietzow2026} identify sparse late attention heads governing prior override; \citet{seeingoverrides2026} causally steer such conflicts. These results are not in tension with ours: in image-vs.-parametric-prior conflicts the competing signal lives in the weights, whereas in our image-vs.-external-text conflicts the competing signal is delivered in the prompt and is carried by the text stream. Our cross-image result refines the picture further: even the \emph{image's} decision-relevant signal migrates into text-stream positions over depth in \gemma{}, suggesting the locus question is jointly determined by conflict type, depth, and architecture.

\paragraph{Emotion and affect in multimodal models.}
EMOTIC and related corpora have been used to benchmark VLM emotion recognition \citep{kosti2019, etesam2024, agarwal2026}, and emotion conflicts across audio-visual modalities have been benchmarked \citep{mosear2025, emomm2026}. VEENA \citep{veena2026} identifies emotion circuits in LVLMs and intervenes to improve recognition; it studies congruent emotion processing with no modality conflict and no valence asymmetry. In text-only LLMs, \citet{tak2025} localize appraisal representations and steer emotion inference causally (our methodological foundation), and \citet{negbeforepos2026} report asymmetric valence processing across layers, an LLM-internal precedent that our cross-modal, behavioral-override setting extends.

\paragraph{Mechanistic interpretability of VLMs.}
Activation patching and causal tracing are established tools \citep{meng2022, patchingbestpractices, attributionpatching}, and cross-modal information flow has been traced in VLMs: image information transfers into text/query positions in early-to-middle layers \citep{basu2024, whatsintheimage2025, narrowgate2024, towardsinterp2024}, and middle-layer last-token attention matters for cross-modal aggregation \citep{fcct2026}. Our cross-image migration result (\S\ref{sec:crosspatch}) is consistent with this literature and extends it to affective content under controlled valence contrasts; our contribution is the conflict-specific decomposition, not the existence of image-to-text transfer. \citet{nikankin2025} find largely disjoint circuits for analogous visual and textual tasks; our claims are compatible, since we posit a shared, causally manipulable valence-sensitive channel within the language backbone, not a single modality-agnostic circuit. Work on attention sinks and delimiter tokens \citep{xiao2024, activedormant2024} and on safety signals concentrating in assistant header tokens \citep{ada2025} anticipates structural tokens as aggregation sites; our carrier in \gemma{} is the assistant-turn boundary and not the BOS sink, though we flag explicitly that a generic-bottleneck interpretation is not yet excluded (\S\ref{sec:patching}).

\paragraph{Human affective science.}
Negativity dominance \citep{rozin2001} and ``bad is stronger than good'' \citep{baumeister2001} are foundational asymmetries in human evaluation, and situational sentences modulate facial-affect perception \citep{aviezer2008, aviezer2012}. We use this literature as motivation; whether VLM valence asymmetries share anything computational with the human construct is an open question our operationalization (cross-modal override) does not settle.

\paragraph{Steering vectors.}
Difference-of-means steering is causally effective but its interpretation requires care \citep{steeringunreliability2025, steeringnonident2026}. We pre-empt standard concerns with norm-matched random nulls and a non-valence specificity control, and we deliberately describe our steering result as causal \emph{outcome control} under conflict, not as re-weighting of the competing cues (\S\ref{sec:arbitration}).

\section{Experimental framework}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Models.}
Primary model: \texttt{google/gemma-3-4b-it} (34 layers, $d_{\text{model}}{=}2560$; SigLIP tower pooled to 256 image tokens), accessed via TransformerBridge in bf16. Generality models, spanning three vision-language unification designs: \qwen{} (native dynamic-resolution ViT, unrelated chat template; raw HuggingFace hooks), \llava{} (frozen CLIP ViT through a linear projector, unpooled patch tokens), and \gemmabig{} (scale within family). All measurements are single forward passes with no generation.

\paragraph{Behavioral readouts.}
Closed-vocabulary \emph{behavioral valence} $= P(\text{positive}) - P(\text{negative})$ over 13 single-token emotion labels at the last prompt token (label set and polarity mapping in Appendix~\ref{app:contexts}; on \llava{}'s tokenizer the labels are multi-token and are scored via a per-model token-id map). For cross-model comparisons we use a calibration-free \emph{override rate}: the fraction of conflict trials where the argmax emotion's valence category follows the text rather than the image. Behavioral valence is bounded and negatively skewed in \gemma{}, so only relative effects are interpreted, saturation is handled by head-room normalization and by the override rate, and an unbounded logit-margin robustness check is left as future work (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Internal readout.}
A linear \emph{pleasantness} probe (ridge regression) trained on crowd-enVENT \citep{troiano2023} text activations at \texttt{attn\_out} layer 18, last token, following \citet{tak2025}; frozen after text training and never re-fit on image data. Applied under image conditioning it tracks EMOTIC ground-truth valence at $\rho = 0.507$ (\S\ref{sec:foundations}). We refer to a valence-sensitive subspace/channel, not \emph{the} valence representation: notably, the probe direction reads but does not steer, while the difference-of-means direction steers.

\paragraph{Stimuli.}
Images: the 75 highest- and 75 lowest-valence person annotations from the EMOTIC test split (150 persons over 121 distinct images). EMOTIC is annotated per person and we feed whole images without bounding boxes, so target ambiguity is a real concern that we quantify: exactly one of the 121 images contains annotated persons in both extreme groups, and excluding it leaves the headline contrast essentially unchanged ($+0.330 \to +0.312$ on the minimal-pair bank); marked-person reruns remain future work (\S\ref{sec:limitations}). Contexts: (i) a \emph{full bank} of 6 positive, 6 negative, and 2 neutral generic one-sentence contexts, and (ii) a \emph{minimal-pair bank} of 6 token-matched pairs flipping only the valence-bearing word (verified token-identical on the Gemma tokenizer; Appendix~\ref{app:contexts}). Contexts are inserted as \texttt{Context: \{ctx\}} into a fixed prompt asking \emph{``What single emotion is this person feeling?''}, 15 conditions per image (2{,}250 forwards per bank). All effects are measured against the \emph{neutral-context} baseline: adding any framing sentence, even a neutral one, shifts both readouts relative to a no-context prompt (Appendix~\ref{app:nocontext}), so the no-context condition is excluded and only context polarity varies. A single-sentence pilot that produced misleading conclusions motivates the bank design (Appendix~\ref{app:pilot}).

\section{A shared cross-modal valence readout and a causal handle}
\label{sec:foundations}

Full details in Appendix~\ref{app:foundations}. Following \citet{tak2025}, ridge probes for six appraisal dimensions peak mid-network (layers 17--21 of 34) at the MHSA output, far above shuffled baselines; pleasantness is strongest (val $r^2 = 0.641$ at layer 18), and layer 18 is fixed as the readout/steering site. Applied unchanged to image-conditioned activations, the frozen pleasantness probe tracks EMOTIC valence at Spearman $\rho = +0.507$ ($n{=}7{,}280$; polarity AUC $0.898$), clearing a 100-draw norm-matched random-direction null ($p{=}0.010$), and retains a unique contribution after controlling jointly for neutral and rich image captions (semipartial $\rho = +0.201$, $p{<}0.001$), so the transfer is not merely verbalization. A difference-of-means pleasantness direction estimated purely from text, injected at layer 18 under image input, moves behavioral valence monotonically (slope $+0.329$; unpleasantness $-0.309$), ${\sim}12\times$ a random null and ${\sim}4.5\times$ a non-valence specificity control. Image and text thus both write into a shared, causally manipulable valence-sensitive channel in the language backbone; upstream computation may be modality-specific \citep{nikankin2025}.

\subsection{The asymmetry: the full factorial picture}
\label{sec:asymmetry}

Table~\ref{tab:factorial} reports the complete image-polarity $\times$ context-polarity matrix. The \textbf{primary behavioral contrast is the mirror contrast} between the two incongruent cells: negative context moves positive-image judgments by $-0.598$, while positive context moves negative-image judgments by $+0.333$. The magnitude difference is $+0.265$ (bootstrap 95\% CI $[+0.095, +0.431]$; Mann-Whitney $p = 0.005$), a ${\sim}1.8\times$ ratio, and it survives head-room normalization (normalized pulls $0.632$ vs.\ $0.259$), so it is not a floor artifact. In categorical terms, negative context overrides a positive image on $84\%$ of trials while positive context overrides a negative image on $21\%$ (gap $+64\%$, 95\% CI clustered over images $[+55, +72]$).

A second, distinct comparison is \emph{within} the positive-image row, where head-room around the neutral baseline ($-0.058$, mid-scale) is symmetric: negative and positive contexts differ $3.58\times$ in displacement from neutral ($-0.598$ vs.\ $+0.167$). We emphasize that this within-row ratio compares an incongruent against a \emph{congruent} condition; it quantifies how unequal the two polarities' leverage is on the same image, not the mirror override, and we report it separately from the mirror contrast. The within-row ratio can also mislead in isolation: on \llava{} it reads $3.06\times$ purely from ceiling/floor geometry while the mirror contrast is null (\S\ref{sec:models}).

\subsection{Valence, not event semantics: token-matched minimal pairs}
\label{sec:minimal}

The full bank's text-only control shows the two banks have similar mean strength with no image present ($+0.706$ vs.\ $-0.752$; we do not claim statistical equivalence from a non-significant test at $n{=}6$ sentences per polarity). But similar mean strength does not rule out the strongest surviving alternative: ``funeral'' and ``championship'' engage different event semantics with different emotion-label diagnosticity. We therefore built a bank of six \emph{minimal pairs} that hold sentence structure and wording constant and flip only the valence-bearing token, verified token-identical on the Gemma tokenizer so the readout position is byte-identical within each pair: won$\leftrightarrow$lost, best$\leftrightarrow$worst, got$\leftrightarrow$lost, wonderful$\leftrightarrow$devastating, celebration$\leftrightarrow$memorial, joyful$\leftrightarrow$heartbreaking.

\paragraph{The strictest test, reported honestly.}
Minimal pairs admit a within-item, within-event paired contrast: per image and per pair, $|\Delta_{\rm neg}| - |\Delta_{\rm pos}|$ vs.\ that image's neutral baseline, on positive images. Within matched events, the asymmetry is carried by the \emph{consistency} of negative framing rather than by uniformly larger negative magnitudes. The category-level override gap and mirror contrast remain the primary results; this paired test is a stricter check that refines the phenomenon's shape.

\paragraph{Cross-modal amplification.}
With no image, the banks are near-symmetric; with a positive image, only $24\%$ of the positive-text effect survives while $80\%$ of the negative-text effect does. The image selectively suppresses \emph{agreeing} framing. We present this as a strong descriptive interaction; a formal crossed statistical test that simultaneously propagates uncertainty over images and context sentences is future work (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Mechanism: a shared text-stream channel}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\subsection{The divergence emerges mid-stack}
\label{sec:layerwise}

Using the frozen probe weight as a fixed direction applied at every layer (a logit-lens-style diagnostic; the noise floor is the raw mean-separation threshold $0.125$ estimated from the pre-divergence layers, and onset/peak are gated for scale and readout sign), the positive-vs.-negative context divergence on the last token first clears the floor at ${\sim}$layer 13 of 34 ($|d| = 0.21$) and amplifies ${\sim}7\times$ through the back half, peaking near layer 28 ($|d| = 1.47$). We read this as ``detectable mid-stack under this lens,'' not as the creation site. An attention knockout at the readout layer is consistent with indirect routing: the last token attends ${\sim}88\%$ to template and question tokens, ${\sim}3.5\%$ to the context, and the direct context contribution is ${\sim}6\%$.

\subsection{Same-image patching: where the context-polarity signal is carried}
\label{sec:patching}

With the \emph{same image} held constant (donor $=$ positive-context run, recipient $=$ negative-context run), we overwrite the recipient's residual stream over layers 13--17 per position-aligned token group and measure recovery toward the positive readout. The token whose state is used for scoring is itself \emph{excluded} from every patched group, so recovery at the assistant-turn boundary is not tautological. Results replicate across two context pairs (Table~\ref{tab:patching}).

We state the conclusions narrowly. \textbf{(1)} This experiment localizes where information \emph{distinguishing positive from negative context} travels; by itself it does not explain why the negative direction dominates behaviorally, and a symmetric model could show the same decomposition. \textbf{(2)} The ${\sim}0\%$ image-token recovery is partly guaranteed by causal ordering: the image precedes the context in the prompt, so image-position states cannot condition on it. We therefore treat this row as a consistency check, not as evidence that the model ``chooses'' not to rewrite visual representations; a context-before-image ordering variant is the direct test and is future work. \textbf{(3)} The assistant-turn-boundary concentration ($57$--$65\%$; BOS and prefix $0\%$) is where preceding prompt information would be expected to accumulate in a causal LM, so a generic answer-preparation-bottleneck interpretation is not excluded; a matched non-affective contrast run through the same decomposition is the missing control. \textbf{(4)} The ${\sim}13$--$15\%$ not recovered by aligned text is an \emph{unrecovered residual under the aligned-position intervention}, not an estimate of the context tokens' causal share: recovery is nonlinear and non-additive (Qwen exhibits super-additivity). Finally, the full-bank context sentences differ in token length across donor/recipient, so aligned positions sit at different absolute offsets; the token-identical minimal pairs remove this confound and re-running the patching on them is planned.

\subsection{Cross-image patching: visual valence migrates into the text stream}
\label{sec:crosspatch}

The mirror question is where the \emph{image's own} valence lives. Holding the context fixed (neutral) and varying the image (donor $=$ positive image, recipient $=$ negative image) makes the two runs byte-identical in \texttt{input\_ids}, so every position, including the context, is 1:1 patchable. Visual valence \emph{originates} in the image tokens and \emph{broadcasts into the text-stream positions over depth}: by the late band the image tokens no longer carry the decision-relevant signal and the text-stream states do. This corrects the natural (and wrong) reading of \S\ref{sec:patching} that the image's valence stays confined to image tokens, and it is consistent with the balanced integration coefficients and the image-to-text transfer literature \citep{basu2024, whatsintheimage2025}. The resulting picture is a \emph{shared-channel competition}: both the image's valence and the context's valence are read out from the same downstream text-stream states, and in \gemma{}/\qwen{} negative language wins the competition within that channel; the image loses not because it is trapped but because it is out-competed at the point of aggregation. A confirmatory run holding a \emph{negative} context fixed restates the dominance behaviorally, the image-driven valence gap collapses from $0.87$ to $0.08$, but that same collapse makes late-band recovery denominators near-zero, so whether the image still broadcasts \emph{under} competition could not be localized (mid-band probe recovery from image tokens remains high, $85\%$, CI $[51, 150]$); we report this as open.

\label{sec:robustness}

A candidate explanation is representational: LLaVA's unpooled, frozen-CLIP visual representation weights the image heavily enough that text cannot commandeer the readout. Whatever the cause, the correct scope is that valence-asymmetric conflict resolution holds for the two modern pooled/native-ViT designs tested and not for the older linear-projector design; it is a property of (model, conflict) pairs, not of VLMs as a class. The coarse mechanism replicates on \qwen{} (image-token patching $0\%$ for the context delta; carrier distributed across the text stream, all-text $65\%$, rather than concentrated at Gemma's turn boundary); mechanism experiments were not run on \llava{}, where there is no dominance to localize.

\subsection{Prompt robustness}
\label{sec:prompts}

Varying only the user-turn phrasing across six natural rephrasings with scoring fixed (13{,}500 forwards), the override gap never flips and every CI clears zero: $+46\%$ to $+74\%$, mean $+58\%$; the base prompt is mid-pack (Table in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}). The prompt sweep reuses the same context bank, so it establishes robustness to phrasing, not to context sampling.

\subsection{Scale}
\label{sec:scale}

From \gemma{} to \gemmabig{}, the gap remains large ($+51\%$, CI $[+0.42, +0.59]$); negative override rises ($84\% \to 93\%$) while positive override more than doubles ($19\% \to 42\%$), so the larger model is more text-driven in both directions. The two gap CIs overlap and the gap metric is ceiling-compressed at $93\%$, so we do not claim the asymmetry changes with scale (Appendix~\ref{app:scale}).

\subsection{Steering causally shifts conflict outcomes}
\label{sec:arbitration}

On the incongruent cells, injecting the text-derived pleasantness $\Delta\mu$ at layer 18 drives behavioral valence through the conflict at slope $+0.215$ per unit $\beta$ (${\sim}65\%$ of the no-conflict slope $+0.329$), with the upstream frozen probe invariant by construction. We interpret this as causal \emph{outcome control}: a direction learned purely from text changes the model's judgment in an ongoing cross-modal conflict, which no verbalization account explains. We deliberately do not claim the steering re-weights the competing cues (``arbitration''), since a generic output-valence direction could produce the same translation of the final score; testing whether steering changes the override rates or the cue coefficients themselves is the natural follow-up.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{A shared-channel account.}
The revised mechanistic picture is more interesting than the one we set out to test. Both cues converge on the same downstream text-stream states: the context's polarity signal is carried there (same-image patching), and the image's valence migrates there over depth (cross-image patching). Conflict is then resolved \emph{within} this shared channel, and in Gemma/Qwen negative language wins the competition. What the present experiments do not yet establish is the computation that privileges the negative direction inside the channel: our patching localizes where polarity information travels, not why one polarity dominates, and the per-pair analysis suggests the behavioral shape is ``negative framing is reliable, positive framing is event-dependent.'' Normalized neutral$\leftrightarrow$polarity patching in both directions is the experiment that would separate asymmetric mediation from asymmetric input.

\paragraph{Why would LLaVA differ?}
The boundary condition constrains explanations of the asymmetry. Accounts that locate it purely in language-side training statistics (negative words are more diagnostic) must explain why LLaVA, whose language backbone is also trained on such statistics, shows none of it. The natural hypothesis is that the unification design sets the image's weight in the shared channel: LLaVA's unpooled projection keeps the image dominant enough that text cannot override in either direction, while pooled/native-ViT designs admit textual override, which then exhibits the valence asymmetry. Testing intermediate designs (e.g., LLaVA-NeXT, OneVision) would map the boundary.

\paragraph{Relation to parametric-conflict mechanisms.}
Image-vs.-parametric-knowledge conflicts localize causal load to image tokens \citep{nooralahzadeh2026, lietzow2026}; our external-text conflicts localize the context delta to text states, and even visual valence migrates text-ward by the readout. Conflict type, depth, and architecture jointly determine locus; treating any single localization as universal is unlikely to survive.

\paragraph{Implications.}
Practically, on affect-sensitive tasks a single negative sentence overrides the visual assessment of a Gemma- or Qwen-class VLM at rates near $90\%$, the effect strengthens on the text side with scale, and model choice matters: an image-anchored design like LLaVA behaves qualitatively differently under identical conflicts. The localization results suggest concrete monitoring hooks (the turn-boundary states in Gemma-class models) where steering demonstrably changes outcomes.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Mechanism scope.} Our patching localizes context-polarity information, not the computation that makes the negative direction win; the normalized neutral$\leftrightarrow$polarity patching (both directions) has not been run. The ${\sim}0\%$ image-token result for the context delta is partly dictated by causal ordering (image precedes context); the context-before-image ordering variant has not been run. The assistant-turn-boundary concentration lacks a matched non-affective contrast control, so a generic-bottleneck reading is open; the boundary group excludes the scoring token, but the control is still needed. Full-bank patching pairs are not length-matched (positional confound); the token-identical minimal pairs enable a clean rerun that is planned. Broadcast-under-competition could not be localized because dominance itself collapses the measurable gap. \textbf{Statistics.} Uncertainty is propagated over images (121 distinct; the one cross-polarity duplicate image changes the headline contrast by $0.018$) but not simultaneously over context sentences; a crossed two-way bootstrap is future work, partially mitigated by the minimal-pair within-item test. The within-item paired contrast is marginal ($p = 0.052$), and two positive pair-members read weak text-only and should be revised. All runs use a single seed. \textbf{Measurement.} Behavioral valence is a bounded probability score; head-room normalization and the calibration-free override rate address saturation, but an unbounded logit-margin replication has not been run. EMOTIC persons are fed as whole images without target marking; the duplicate-image check is reassuring but marked-person or cropped reruns are the direct fix. \textbf{Scope.} One dataset and task family (person-emotion labeling), one context construction style, three architectures with the mechanism probed on one (coarsely on a second). Claims are scoped to controlled image-context emotion judgments on the tested designs; broader conflict domains, tasks, and architectures are open.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Cross-modal conflict resolution in the VLMs we test is valence-asymmetric: negative language changes image-grounded emotion judgments more than positive language does, the asymmetry survives token-matched minimal pairs and appears only in the presence of the image, and it is a property of the unification architecture rather than of VLMs as a class, present in Gemma-3 and Qwen3-VL, absent in image-anchored LLaVA-1.5. Mechanistically, both cues converge on a shared text-stream readout channel in \gemma{}: the context's polarity signal is carried there, the image's valence migrates there over depth, and a text-derived valence direction injected into the channel causally shifts conflict outcomes. The valence of competing evidence, and the architecture that merges the modalities, are first-class variables in multimodal arbitration.

\section{Patching methodology}
\label{app:patching}

\paragraph{Same-image design (\S\ref{sec:patching}).}
Donor $=$ positive-context run, recipient $=$ negative-context run, same image; \texttt{resid\_post} overwritten over layers 13--17 for one position-aligned group at a time; recovery $= (\text{patched} - \text{neg})/(\text{pos} - \text{neg})$ at the L18 probe readout. Groups: image (256 tokens), question, BOS, user-turn prefix delimiters, assistant-turn suffix (\texttt{<end\_of\_turn>\textbackslash n<start\_of\_turn>model}); the scoring token itself is excluded from all groups. 60 images per pair; pairs: championship/funeral and wonderful/devastating. Behavioral-valence recovery follows the same ordering but noisier (all-text $73$--$78\%$). Full-bank sentences differ in token length across donor/recipient, so aligned groups sit at different absolute positions; this positional confound is removed by the token-identical minimal pairs, on which the patching rerun is planned.

\paragraph{Cross-image design (\S\ref{sec:crosspatch}).}
Donor $=$ positive image, recipient $=$ negative image, context fixed, so \texttt{input\_ids} are byte-identical and every position is patchable; 60 pairs; bands 0--12, 13--17, 18--28; recovery on behavioral valence, with the L18 probe corroborating where valid (the probe taps \texttt{attn\_out} L18 computed from L17, so probe recovery is invariant-by-construction for the 18--28 band and is gated out there). Sanity: patching all positions except the scoring token recovers $100\%$ / $91\%$ / $79\%$ by band. The negative-context confirmatory run collapses the image-driven gap to $0.08$, making late-band recovery denominators near-zero; its CIs are reported but not interpreted.

\paragraph{Qwen port.}
Raw HuggingFace hooks, behavioral-valence recovery, 60 positive images, broad mid+late band; Qwen distributes the carrier (question $12\%$, turn scaffold $6\%$, all aligned text $65\%$, super-additive), consistent with redundant copies across text positions.

\section{Compute}

All experiments are inference-only single forward passes (no gradients, no generation) on one A100-40GB-class GPU in bf16. Forward-pass counts: full-bank base pass 2{,}250; minimal-pair base pass 2{,}250; text-only controls 15 per bank per model; arbitration sweep 1{,}050; prompt sweep 13{,}500; scaling 2 $\times$ 2{,}250; \qwen{} and \llava{} base passes 2{,}250 each plus text-only; layerwise sweep one cached forward per image per condition pair (51 paired images); same-image patching 60 images $\times$ 2 pairs $\times$ 7 groups; cross-image patching 60 pairs $\times$ 3 bands $\times$ 6 groups plus the negative-context confirmatory run. \todo{Add wall-clock totals per run from the session logs for camera-ready; the full project including preliminary and failed runs used more compute than the reported experiments.}
