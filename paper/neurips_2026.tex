\begin{abstract}
When an image and its accompanying text disagree, a vision-language model (VLM) must decide which to follow. Prior work asks \emph{which modality} wins such conflicts; we ask whether the answer depends on \emph{what the text says}. We pair emotionally positive and negative photographs of people with short context sentences of the opposite valence, and measure how far each cue moves the model's emotion judgment relative to a neutral-context baseline. In \qwen{}, conflict resolution is asymmetric: a negative sentence overrides a positive image on $76\%$ of trials, while a positive sentence overrides a negative image on $37\%$ (gap $+39\%$). Holding the event fixed and flipping a single valence word --- won$\leftrightarrow$lost, wonderful$\leftrightarrow$devastating --- sharpens the effect rather than removing it, and all six such pairs agree, with negative framing moving the judgment four to five times as far as its positive counterpart. The asymmetry therefore tracks valence, not event content. We evaluate four VLMs on three readouts --- a categorical override rate, a bounded graded score, and an unbounded log-odds margin --- and find the asymmetry is present in three of them but that \emph{which readout registers it most strongly varies by model}, tracking how each model uses the bounded scale. It is absent on \llava{}, whose two conflict directions are statistically indistinguishable. We report image-clustered and crossed (image $\times$ sentence) confidence intervals throughout; with six sentences per polarity the design establishes robustness to image sampling but not to sentence sampling, and we say so. Activation patching in \gemma{} shows the context effect is carried entirely by text-token states --- image tokens recover none of it --- while the image's own valence is recoverable from image tokens early in the network and from text states later. At those states, a valence direction learned purely from text causally shifts image-conditioned output. Multimodal conflict thus depends on the valence of the evidence, the architecture, and the scale on which the outcome is measured.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Show a vision-language model a smiling face alongside the sentence \emph{``This was taken at her best friend's funeral,''} and it must decide which signal to follow. Prior work shows that this choice varies with the task, model, prompt, relevance of the text, and relation between the two modalities \citep{deng2025blindfaith, mixedsignals2025, contextvqa2026, signpost2026}. One question remains open: under otherwise matched conditions, does positive text pull the model as strongly as negative text?

Human affective science suggests it should not. Negativity dominance --- the finding that mixtures of negative and positive cues are judged more negatively than their parts predict --- is among the most replicated results in psychology \citep{rozin2001, baumeister2001}, and context sentences are known to reshape how people read facial emotion \citep{aviezer2008, aviezer2012}. Whether VLMs show an analogous asymmetry, in which models, and through what computation, has not been established.

Our paradigm is simple. We take photographs of people from the extremes of the EMOTIC valence distribution \citep{kosti2019} and pair each with one-sentence contexts --- positive, negative, and neutral --- inserted into a fixed prompt that asks what the person is feeling. Both conflict directions are run (negative sentence on a positive image, and the mirror), and every effect is measured against the neutral-context baseline. We read the model's judgment two ways: behaviorally, from the probabilities it assigns to emotion words; and internally, with a linear valence probe trained only on text \citep{tak2025} and applied, frozen, under image input.

Five findings emerge.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Conflict resolution is valence-asymmetric (\qwen{}).} A negative sentence overrides a positive image on $76\%$ of trials; a positive sentence overrides a negative image on $37\%$ (gap $+39\%$, 95\% CI clustered over images $[+30, +49]$; crossed over images \emph{and} sentences $[+6, +67]$). \qwen{} is also the only model of the four that weights the two cues comparably ($|\betatxt|/|\betaimg| = 1.14$), so its asymmetry cannot be attributed to it ignoring the image.
  \item \textbf{It tracks valence, not event content.} Six token-matched minimal pairs --- identical sentences differing in one valence word --- \emph{sharpen} every metric relative to the full bank (override gap $+41\%$; mirror contrast $+0.478$). The within-item contrast, holding photograph and event fixed, is $+1.148$ (CI $[+0.94, +1.34]$, $p < 0.001$), and all six pairs point the same way at ratios of $4.3$ to $5.5$. On this bank both the override gap and the mirror contrast clear zero even when sentences are resampled alongside photographs.
  \item \textbf{The asymmetry is present in three of four models, but which readout registers it depends on the model.} We report a categorical override rate, a bounded graded score, and an unbounded log-odds margin for every model (Table~\ref{tab:models}). All three agree on \llava{}, where the effect is absent. On the other three they disagree in a way that tracks how each model uses the bounded scale: \qwen{} saturates it, so its margin readout understates the effect, while \gemma{} sits near the ceiling on positive images, so its bounded readout does. On the full bank \emph{no model clears zero on all three readouts under the crossed bootstrap}; we report that rather than selecting a readout per model.
  \item \textbf{Two architectural explanations are ruled out.} The asymmetry is \emph{not} a property of the vision-language connector: \llavanext{} uses the same linear-projector family as \llava{} and does show it (gap $+18.7\%$; mirror contrast $+0.413$, crossed CI $[+0.23,+0.59]$). It is also not driven by the visual token budget: a resolution sweep on \qwen{} with identical weights is flat from 128 to 262 image tokens ($+41.8\%$, $+37.4\%$, $+38.6\%$), and a budget-matched cross-model pair differs by 27 points in the opposite direction. We report the variation across models as unexplained rather than substituting a third architectural story.
  \item \textbf{Both cues reach the same downstream text states (\gemma{}).} Activation patching shows the context effect is carried by text-token states and \emph{not} by image tokens, which recover $0\%$ of it. The image's own valence is recoverable from image tokens early in the network ($100\%$ at layers 0--12) and increasingly from text positions later ($63\%$ at layers 18--28). A valence direction learned purely from text, injected at these states, causally shifts image-conditioned output.
\end{enumerate}

Together, these experiments establish the behavioral asymmetry in the model that shows it most cleanly, map how it varies across architectures and readouts, rule out two explanations for that variation, and trace where the competing signals travel inside \gemma{}. Two limits are load-bearing and we state them up front: with six sentences per polarity the design establishes robustness to \emph{image} sampling but not to \emph{sentence} sampling (\S\ref{sec:models}), and the mechanism experiments are run on \gemma{}, whose own behavioral asymmetry is the weakest of the three positive models (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

We scope our claims carefully. We do not claim to discover that text can override vision \citep{deng2025blindfaith, contextvqa2026}, nor the first mechanistic study of multimodal conflict \citep{seeingoverrides2026}, nor the first study of multimodal emotion conflict \citep{mosear2025}. And while the behavioral pattern is consistent with negativity dominance in humans, we treat it as an analogue, not the same construct.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Modality conflict in VLMs.}
Several lines of work create mismatched image-text pairs and ask which modality the model follows. \citet{deng2025blindfaith} show that VLMs disproportionately trust text when it has been corrupted, while \citet{mixedsignals2025} find that the preferred modality varies with task complexity and model. Contradictory persuasive text can override clear visual evidence across 11 VLMs \citep{contextvqa2026}, including in geolocation \citep{signpost2026}. Misleading instructions can also become \emph{more} influential when an image is present \citep{camel2025}. These studies show that image-text conflict is real and can matter for safety. They examine factors such as relevance, task difficulty, and the relation between the cues, but do not isolate whether matched positive and negative text exert different pull. We test that question in both conflict directions and across model designs.

\paragraph{Different kinds of conflict.}
A complementary line of work studies conflicts between visual input and knowledge stored in the model's weights. \citet{nooralahzadeh2026} find that image tokens carry nearly all of the effect when a model must override such prior knowledge; \citet{lietzow2026} identify a small set of late attention heads involved in that override; and \citet{seeingoverrides2026} steer the resulting decisions. Our setting differs because the competing text appears directly in the prompt. In \gemma{}, even the image's decision-relevant signal becomes recoverable from text positions over depth. The location of conflict resolution may therefore depend on the kind of conflict, the network depth, and the model design.

\paragraph{Emotion and affect in multimodal models.}
EMOTIC and related datasets are widely used to test VLM emotion recognition \citep{kosti2019, etesam2024, agarwal2026}. Other benchmarks study emotion conflicts between audio and vision \citep{mosear2025, emomm2026}. VEENA \citep{veena2026} identifies and intervenes on emotion-related circuits in large VLMs, but studies matching rather than conflicting cues and does not test valence asymmetry. In text-only LLMs, \citet{tak2025} locate appraisal-related information and steer emotion judgments, providing the method we build on. \citet{negbeforepos2026} report that positive and negative valence are processed differently across layers. We extend that question to behavioral conflicts between images and text.

\paragraph{Mechanistic interpretability of VLMs.}
Activation patching and causal tracing are established tools for locating information that affects a model's output \citep{meng2022, patchingbestpractices, attributionpatching}. Prior VLM studies show that image information moves into text or query positions in early-to-middle layers \citep{basu2024, whatsintheimage2025, narrowgate2024, towardsinterp2024}, and that middle-layer attention at the last token helps combine the modalities \citep{fcct2026}. Our cross-image result (\S\ref{sec:crosspatch}) agrees with this work. Our contribution is to trace the two signals during a conflict, not to establish image-to-text transfer itself. \citet{nikankin2025} find largely separate circuits for similar visual and textual tasks. That result is compatible with ours: different upstream computations can still feed information into the same downstream states. Other work finds that delimiter and assistant-header tokens can collect information from across a prompt \citep{xiao2024, activedormant2024, zhang2026anydepth}. In \gemma{} the context difference is carried by text-token states, but it is split about evenly between the question tokens and the tokens before the model's answer (\S\ref{sec:patching}), so our data do not single out the turn scaffold and we do not claim support for that account.

\paragraph{Human affective science.}
Negativity dominance \citep{rozin2001} and ``bad is stronger than good'' \citep{baumeister2001} are foundational asymmetries in human evaluation, and situational sentences modulate facial-affect perception \citep{aviezer2008, aviezer2012}. We use this literature as motivation; whether VLM valence asymmetries share anything computational with the human construct is an open question our operationalization (cross-modal override) does not settle.

\paragraph{Steering vectors.}
Difference-of-means steering can change model outputs, but that does not by itself reveal what computation changed \citep{steeringunreliability2025, steeringnonident2026}. We compare our valence direction with random directions of the same norm and with a direction for a different appraisal. We therefore claim only that steering changes the outcome under conflict, not that it changes how strongly the model weights each cue (\S\ref{sec:arbitration}).

\section{Experimental framework}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Task and stimuli.}
Each trial shows the model a photograph of a person and a one-sentence context. A fixed prompt asks, \emph{``What single emotion is this person feeling?''} (exact template in Appendix~\ref{app:contexts}). We use the 75 most positive and 75 most negative person annotations from the EMOTIC test split \citep{kosti2019}: 150 people across 121 distinct images.

The contexts come from two banks. The \emph{full bank} contains 6 positive, 6 negative, and 2 neutral generic sentences, such as ``This photo was taken moments after they won the championship.'' The \emph{minimal-pair bank} contains 6 sentence pairs that differ by one valence-bearing word, such as won$\leftrightarrow$lost. The two members of each pair also have the same number of Gemma tokens, so their readout positions match (Appendix~\ref{app:contexts}). Crossing images with contexts gives 15 conditions per image and 2{,}250 forward passes per bank.

EMOTIC labels each person, but we give the model the whole image without a bounding box. We therefore checked whether the same image could contribute people to both valence groups. This occurs for exactly one of the 121 images. Excluding it barely changes the headline contrast ($+0.330 \to +0.312$ on the minimal-pair bank). Reruns with the target person marked remain future work (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Design decisions.}
Two choices matter for interpreting the results. First, we measure every effect against a \emph{neutral-context} baseline rather than a prompt with no context. Adding any framing sentence, even a neutral one, changes the model's judgment (Appendix~\ref{app:nocontext}). The neutral baseline keeps the presence of a sentence fixed while its polarity changes. Second, we use banks of contexts rather than a single positive and negative sentence. An early single-sentence pilot produced conclusions that did not survive the bank design (Appendix~\ref{app:pilot}).

\paragraph{Models.}
We evaluate four VLMs spanning distinct vision-language designs. \qwen{} (native dynamic-resolution ViT) is our primary behavioral model: it is the only one of the four that weights the two cues comparably, so its conflict results are not confounded by it under-using one modality. \gemma{} (34 layers, $d_{\text{model}}{=}2560$; SigLIP vision tower pooled to 256 image tokens) is our primary \emph{mechanism} model, because the frozen text-trained probe of \S\ref{sec:foundations} was fit on its activations. \llava{} (frozen CLIP ViT through a linear projector, unpooled patch tokens) and \llavanext{} (the same projector family at a much larger token budget) complete the comparison. All models are run in bf16 through the standard HuggingFace implementation, with no intermediate wrapper. All measurements are single forward passes; nothing is generated. Compute details are in Appendix~\ref{app:compute}.

\paragraph{Behavioral readouts.}
We read the model's judgment from the probabilities it assigns to 13 emotion words at the last prompt token (\emph{joy}, \emph{sadness}, \emph{fear}, and others listed in Appendix~\ref{app:contexts}). \emph{Behavioral valence} is $P(\text{positive labels}) - P(\text{negative labels})$, a graded score in $[-1, 1]$. For comparisons across models, we also use the \emph{override rate}: the fraction of conflict trials in which the top emotion label follows the text rather than the image. This categorical measure is easier to compare across models because each model uses the bounded valence scale differently. When a score is near its floor or ceiling, we also divide the shift by the remaining room in that direction. We interpret only relative shifts; repeating the analysis with an unbounded difference between positive and negative logits remains future work (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Internal readout.}
Alongside behavior, we read the model's internal state with a linear \emph{pleasantness} probe. Following \citet{tak2025}, we train a ridge regression on text-only activations from crowd-enVENT \citep{troiano2023} at the last token of \texttt{attn\_out} layer 18. We then freeze the probe and apply it to image inputs without re-fitting it. Its performance under image input therefore tests whether a text-learned readout transfers across modalities. The probe tracks EMOTIC ground-truth valence at $\rho = 0.510$ (\S\ref{sec:foundations}). We call the feature it reads \emph{valence-sensitive}, rather than claiming it is the model's only valence representation. The probe direction can read valence but cannot steer it; a difference-of-means direction in the same activation space can.

\section{Reading and steering valence across modalities}
\label{sec:foundations}

Before studying conflicts, we establish a cross-modal valence readout and show that it can affect behavior. Full details are in Appendix~\ref{app:foundations}.

\textbf{First, a text-trained probe locates valence-related information.} Following \citet{tak2025}, ridge probes for six appraisal dimensions peak in the middle of the network (layers 17--21 of 34) at the attention output, far above shuffled baselines. Pleasantness is strongest, with validation $r^2 = 0.641$ at layer 18. We therefore fix layer 18 as the readout and steering site for the remaining experiments.

\textbf{Second, the probe transfers to images.} Applied unchanged to image-conditioned activations, it tracks EMOTIC valence at Spearman $\rho = +0.510$ ($n{=}7{,}280$ images scored; polarity AUC $0.912$ on the 440 single-label polar images) and beats all 100 norm-matched random directions ($p{=}0.010$). After accounting for neutral and detailed captions, the image readout still explains unique variation in valence (semipartial $\rho = +0.201$, $p{<}0.001$). Thus, ordinary captioning does not fully explain the transferred signal.

\textbf{Third, a text-derived direction changes image-conditioned behavior.} We estimate the mean activation difference between high- and low-pleasantness text examples and inject that direction at layer 18 under image input. Behavioral valence moves monotonically with the intervention (slope $+0.336$), about $9.6\times$ the norm-matched random control ($-0.035$) and $4.3\times$ the non-valence specificity control ($-0.078$). Together, these results support a valence-sensitive activation space that both modalities can influence. They do not imply that vision and language use the same upstream computation \citep{nikankin2025}.

\section{Valence-asymmetric conflict resolution}
\label{sec:conflict}

\subsection{Do both cues matter? Integration across four models}
\label{sec:integration}

Before asking which cue wins, we check that both cues affect the model. We regress behavioral valence on image valence and context polarity after standardizing both predictors, using only conditions that contain a sentence (Table~\ref{tab:integration}).

Both predictors carry the expected sign in every model, so no model ignores a modality. They differ sharply in balance. \qwen{} is the only model that weights text slightly above image ($|\betatxt|/|\betaimg| = 1.14$); the other three are image-led, at $0.44$ to $0.55$. This matters for interpretation: an override asymmetry in an image-led model is harder to read, because the text is starting from a weaker position. \qwen{} is therefore our primary behavioral model --- its asymmetry cannot be explained by it under-using the image. This balance applies only to the affective task studied here.

\subsection{The asymmetry: the full factorial picture}
\label{sec:asymmetry}

Table~\ref{tab:factorial} reports every combination of image and context polarity for \qwen{}. Our primary comparison is between the two conflict conditions. A negative sentence moves positive-image judgments by $-1.156$. The reverse conflict, a positive sentence on negative images, moves judgments by $+0.762$. We call the difference between these magnitudes the \emph{mirror contrast}. Computed per image against that image's own neutral baseline and then averaged, it is $+0.409$ (bootstrap 95\% CI clustered over images $[+0.230, +0.588]$). Taking it instead as the difference of the cell means in Table~\ref{tab:factorial} gives $+0.394$; we report the per-image form throughout because it is the quantity the confidence intervals are computed on.

Negative images begin closer to the lower bound of the score, so they have less room to move. We account for this by dividing each shift by the available room in that direction. The normalized pulls remain unequal ($0.714$ vs.\ $0.394$, difference $+0.320$), so the result is not produced by the score's lower bound. The categorical result agrees: negative context overrides a positive image on $76\%$ of trials, whereas positive context overrides a negative image on $37\%$, a gap of $+39\%$ (95\% CI clustered over images $[+30, +49]$).

Figure~\ref{fig:conflict} shows this pattern against \llava{}, where the same two conflict directions are symmetric. A second comparison uses only positive images. On these, negative and positive contexts differ by $3.64\times$ in their distance from neutral ($-1.156$ vs.\ $+0.317$). This ratio compares a conflicting context with an \emph{agreeing} context on the same image; it is not the mirror contrast, and we report it separately. On its own it can mislead: \llava{} reaches $3.05\times$ purely through the score's bounds even though its mirror contrast is null (\S\ref{sec:models}). We therefore never use this ratio as evidence on its own.

\paragraph{A caveat that applies to every number in this subsection.}
The confidence intervals above are clustered over images, which treats the six sentences per polarity as fixed. Resampling sentences as well (\S\ref{sec:models}, Table~\ref{tab:models}) widens \qwen{}'s mirror-contrast interval to $[-0.16, +0.91]$, which includes zero; its override gap survives at $[+6, +67]$. With six sentences per polarity the design is well powered for generalization across images and weakly powered for generalization across sentences, because resampling six \emph{unrelated events} perturbs the estimate heavily. The token-matched bank of \S\ref{sec:minimal} removes that event variance and recovers a crossed interval excluding zero on both the override gap and the mirror contrast. We report both intervals everywhere rather than choosing the narrower one.

\subsection{Valence, not event semantics: token-matched minimal pairs}
\label{sec:minimal}

Could the asymmetry come from the sentences rather than their valence? Without an image, the positive and negative banks have similar mean strength, but with six sentences per polarity a non-significant test cannot establish that the banks are equivalent. A sharper concern remains: ``funeral'' and ``championship'' describe different events, and some events may point more directly to particular emotion labels.

We therefore built six \emph{minimal pairs}. Each pair uses the same sentence and changes only one valence-bearing word: won$\leftrightarrow$lost, best$\leftrightarrow$worst, got$\leftrightarrow$lost, wonderful$\leftrightarrow$devastating, celebration$\leftrightarrow$memorial, or joyful$\leftrightarrow$heartbreaking. Each pair also has the same token count under the model's tokenizer, so the readout position does not change between the two members. Because the two members share a context id, the design supports a strict \emph{within-item} test: for a fixed image and a fixed event, compare the negative member's effect against the positive member's, each measured from that image's own neutral baseline, $|\Delta_{\rm neg}| - |\Delta_{\rm pos}|$.

The asymmetry survives, and sharpens (Table~\ref{tab:minimal}). The override gap is $+40.8\%$ against $+39.4\%$ for the full bank, the mirror contrast rises from $+0.409$ to $+0.496$, and the model moves from balanced to text-led ($|\betatxt|/|\betaimg| = 1.97$). Since the minimal bank removes event variation, the effect is not carried by ``funeral'' and ``championship'' denoting different kinds of situations.

The more consequential gain is in the crossed intervals. On the full bank the mirror contrast does not survive sentence resampling ($[-0.16, +0.91]$); on the minimal bank it does ($[+0.11, +0.83]$), and the override gap's crossed interval tightens from a 60-point span to a 46-point span ($[+16.7, +63.2]$). Removing between-sentence event variance is what buys this: the six minimal sentences differ only in valence, so resampling them perturbs the estimate far less than resampling six unrelated events does. The unbounded margin remains negative and straddles zero on both banks ($-1.13$ here), which is the saturation property discussed in \S\ref{sec:models} rather than a failure of this control.

\paragraph{The strictest test.}
The within-item contrast is the tightest form of the claim: same photograph, same event, one word changed. On positive images it is $+1.148$ (bootstrap CI over images $[+0.943, +1.344]$; Wilcoxon $p < 0.001$; $n = 62$ images $\times$ 6 pairs).

Table~\ref{tab:pairs} shows why this is the paper's most robust result. All six pairs point the same way, and they do so at closely similar magnitudes ($+1.10$ to $+1.29$, ratios $4.3$ to $5.5$). The concern raised by the crossed intervals of \S\ref{sec:asymmetry} --- that six sentences per polarity cannot show the effect generalizes across sentences --- is answered here in the only way this design can answer it: not by widening the bank, but by showing that the effect does not depend on which of the six events is used. Every event, taken alone, reproduces it.

Two caveats. Ratios on a bounded scale are inflated when the positive member has little room to move, which is why we report the difference $|\Delta_{\rm neg}| - |\Delta_{\rm pos}|$ as the primary statistic and the ratio only alongside it. And the two neutral contexts are not interchangeable: on positive images ``taken on a weekday'' gives $+0.914$ while ``taken indoors'' gives $+0.344$. We average over both, but the spread is a reminder that ``neutral'' framing is not inert.

\section{Mechanism: where the two signals travel}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\subsection{The difference emerges in the middle layers}
\label{sec:layerwise}

Where in the network do positive- and negative-context runs begin to differ? At each layer, we project the last-token state onto the frozen probe direction. Under this diagnostic, the difference first exceeds the pre-divergence noise level near layer 13 of 34 ($|d| = 0.21$; noise level $0.125$). It then grows by about $7\times$ through the second half of the network and peaks near layer 28 ($|d| = 1.47$). This shows where the difference becomes detectable under the probe; it does not show where the signal is first created.

The context's effect on the final token need not be direct: the final token could read the context words itself, or the context could first reshape other token positions that the final token then reads. The next experiment settles this causally.

\subsection{Same-image patching: where the context-polarity signal is carried}
\label{sec:patching}

Which token positions carry the context's valence signal? We hold the image constant and compare a positive-context run with a negative-context run. For one group of token positions at a time, we replace the negative-context activations over layers 13--17 with the corresponding activations from the positive-context run. We then measure how much the readout moves toward the positive-context value. The final token whose state we score is excluded from every patched group, so the experiment does not recover the answer by directly replacing that state. The result repeats across two context pairs (Table~\ref{tab:patching}); Appendix~\ref{app:patching} gives the full method.

The context difference is carried by downstream text-token states: patching all aligned text recovers $88$--$93\%$ of it, while image tokens and the beginning-of-sequence token recover none. Within the text stream the effect is \emph{distributed}, not concentrated --- the question tokens and the tokens marking the start of the model's answer contribute comparably ($47\%$ vs.\ $46\%$, and $54\%$ vs.\ $40\%$), and their contributions are close to additive. We therefore do not attribute the effect to the turn scaffold specifically. Four limits matter.

\textbf{First,} this experiment locates the positive-versus-negative difference; it does not explain why negative context has the larger behavioral effect. A symmetric model could show the same pattern of information flow. \textbf{Second,} the near-zero recovery from image tokens partly follows from prompt order. The image appears before the context, so image-position states cannot contain information from later context words. We treat this as a consistency check. A direct test would place context before the image. \textbf{Third,} the tokens before the answer may generally collect information from the prompt. Without a matched non-affective control, we cannot tell whether their role is specific to valence. \textbf{Fourth,} the $13$--$15\%$ not recovered by all aligned text is simply the remainder under this intervention. It is not an estimate of the context tokens' causal share, because patching effects need not add linearly. The full-bank sentences also differ in token length, placing aligned groups at different absolute positions. A planned rerun on the token-matched pairs removes this last confound.

\subsection{Cross-image patching: where visual valence can be recovered}
\label{sec:crosspatch}

We next ask where the \emph{image's own} valence can be recovered. We hold the context fixed and neutral, then compare a positive-image run with a negative-image run. Because the text inputs are identical, every text position can be matched exactly. Table~\ref{tab:crosspatch} reports how much of the image-driven difference is recovered when activations are patched over three layer ranges. Behavioral valence is the main readout; the layer-18 probe agrees in the ranges where it is valid.

Early in the network, the image-driven difference is recovered almost entirely from image tokens ($100\%$) and essentially not at all from text positions ($2\%$). Late in the network the ordering reverses: image-token recovery falls to $31\%$ while text-position recovery rises to $63\%$. We interpret this as visual valence becoming \emph{additionally} readable from text-token states over depth, rather than migrating out of the image tokens --- a third of it is still recoverable there at layers 18--28. This agrees with prior evidence of image-to-text transfer \citep{basu2024, whatsintheimage2025} and corrects the idea that visual valence remains confined to image tokens.

In the mid band, where both readouts are valid, they disagree about which group dominates: the layer-18 probe gives image $75\%$ vs.\ text $87\%$, while behavioral valence gives image $96\%$ vs.\ text $74\%$ (non-overlapping intervals). Both come from the same model and inference stack, so this reflects \emph{where} the readout is taken --- at layer 18 versus after sixteen further layers of mixing --- not a measurement error. We report both and draw no conclusion about mid-band ordering.

The same downstream text states can therefore carry information about both the context and the image. This provides a concrete site where the two cues can compete. It does not show whether visual information continues to move into text states while negative context is winning. In a confirmatory run with fixed negative context, the image-driven valence gap shrinks from $0.87$ to $0.08$. That small denominator makes late-layer recovery unstable, so we could not answer the question. Mid-layer image-token recovery remains high at $85\%$ (CI $[51, 150]$), but the wide interval prevents a stronger conclusion.

\section{Generality, robustness, and steering}
\label{sec:robustness}

\subsection{Four vision-language designs, three readouts}
\label{sec:models}

Is the asymmetry a property of VLMs in general, or of particular designs? We run the same conflict experiment on four ways of connecting vision to language. \qwen{} uses a native vision transformer accepting varying resolutions. \gemma{} pools its SigLIP output to 256 image tokens. \llava{} passes unpooled patches from a frozen CLIP tower through a linear projection. \llavanext{} uses that same linear-projector design at a far larger token budget (2{,}147 image tokens).

Because each model uses the bounded valence scale differently, no single readout is neutral. We therefore report three: the categorical \emph{override rate} (\S\ref{sec:setup}), the bounded \emph{mirror contrast}, and an \emph{unbounded log-odds margin}, $\log P(\text{pos})/P(\text{neg})$, which removes the scale's bounds entirely. For each we give an interval clustered over photographs and a crossed interval that resamples photographs \emph{and} context sentences (Table~\ref{tab:models}).

Three results follow.

\textbf{The asymmetry is present in three of four models, absent in the fourth.} \qwen{}, \llavanext{} and \gemma{} each clear zero on at least one readout under the crossed bootstrap, and their mirror contrasts are close in size ($+0.409$, $+0.413$, $+0.405$). \llava{} clears zero on none: its mirror contrast is $+0.016$ and its unbounded margin $+0.05$, both sitting essentially on zero. We describe \llava{} as a null rather than a reversal. Its negative override gap ($-12.6\%$) does not survive sentence resampling, and its two conflict directions have equal head-room-normalized shifts ($0.33/0.33$), so the apparent reversal is scale geometry.

\textbf{No model shows it on all three readouts, and the disagreements are systematic.} \qwen{} clears only on the override rate: its bounded valence saturates at $\pm 1$, which compresses the graded readouts. \gemma{} shows the opposite pattern --- its unbounded margin is the largest of any model ($+4.64$ log-odds) while its override gap does not clear, because its positive-image baseline sits near the ceiling so a large log-odds move rarely crosses a category boundary. This is why we report all three rather than selecting one per model. It also means the size of the effect is not readout-independent, and we make no claim about its magnitude in absolute terms.

\textbf{Two architectural explanations are ruled out.} The first is the connector. \llavanext{} shares \llava{}'s frozen-CLIP linear-projector design and \emph{does} show the asymmetry (mirror contrast $+0.413$, crossed $[+0.23,+0.59]$), so ``linear-projector designs resist textual override'' is false, and we retract that claim. The second is the visual token budget. A resolution sweep on \qwen{} with identical weights is flat across a two-fold change in image tokens (128 tokens: $+41.8\%$; 262 tokens: $+37.4\%$ and $+38.6\%$; all intervals overlapping, image discriminability held at AUC $0.983$--$0.986$), and a budget-matched cross-model pair separates by 27 points in the direction opposite to the hypothesis (\gemma{} at 256 tokens, $+12.2\%$; \qwen{} at 262 tokens, $+39.4\%$). Token budget also cannot explain \llavanext{}, which shows the effect at 2{,}147 image tokens.

We therefore report the variation across models as \emph{unexplained}. Two candidate accounts have been tested and rejected; we prefer to say so rather than substitute a third. A caveat on \llavanext{}: it changes the language backbone (Mistral) relative to \llava{} (Vicuna), so backbone and token budget are not fully separated. The clean comparison, \texttt{llava-v1.6-vicuna-7b}, has not been run (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Mechanism replication.} A coarse version of the patching result also replicates on \qwen{}: image tokens recover none of the context difference, while all text positions together recover $65\%$. \qwen{} spreads the information redundantly --- individual token groups recover little (question $12\%$, turn markers $6\%$) while their union recovers far more than their sum, which is not the case in \gemma{}, where the groups are close to additive (\S\ref{sec:patching}). We did not run mechanism experiments on \llava{}, where there is no asymmetry to explain.

\subsection{Prompt robustness and scale}
\label{sec:prompts}

Two robustness experiments --- a six-way rephrasing sweep of the question and a within-family scale comparison from \gemma{} to \gemmabig{} --- were run on an inference stack we subsequently found computed a different multimodal forward pass from the reference implementation. Their results depended on absolute override rates, the quantity most affected, so we withdraw them rather than report them. Both are re-runs of existing code on the corrected stack and neither is conceptually novel; we report their absence rather than leaving stale numbers in place. \todo{Re-run the prompt sweep (13{,}500 forwards) and the \gemmabig{} scale comparison (2 $\times$ 2{,}250) on the corrected stack, or drop both subsections for the camera-ready.}

What survives from the prompt work is the design point that motivated it: the sweep reuses a single context bank, so it could only ever establish robustness to phrasing of the \emph{question}, never to sampling of the \emph{context sentences}. The crossed intervals in Table~\ref{tab:models} address the latter directly and are the stronger control.

\subsection{Steering causally shifts conflict outcomes}
\label{sec:arbitration}

Finally, we test whether the activation space of \S\ref{sec:foundations} can causally change a judgment during conflict. Injecting the text-derived pleasantness direction at layer 18 moves behavioral valence with a slope of $+0.215$ per unit of steering strength, about $64\%$ of the no-conflict slope ($+0.336$). A direction learned only from text therefore changes the model's output while image and text disagree.

This establishes causal control of the outcome, but not of the competition between the cues: a general output-valence direction could shift the final score without changing either cue's weight. Testing whether steering changes override rates or cue coefficients is the natural next step. \todo{The conflict steering slope ($+0.215$) is the one number in this section still measured on the superseded stack. It is a slope, and the no-conflict slope it is normalized against reproduced to within $2\%$ on re-run ($+0.336$ vs.\ $+0.329$), so we expect it to survive; confirm before camera-ready.}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Where the cues meet.}
The patching experiments point to the same downstream text-token states for both cues. Same-image patching shows that these states carry the context difference. Cross-image patching shows that visual valence becomes recoverable from them over depth. These findings identify a plausible meeting point for image-text conflict. They do not show why negative context has the larger effect. The pair-level results offer one clue: negative framing is consistent, while positive framing depends more on the event. Patching neutral-to-positive and neutral-to-negative changes separately would test whether the asymmetry arises while information is being carried through the network or is already present in the inputs.

\paragraph{Why does \llava{} differ?}
We do not know, and we have ruled out the two explanations we found most plausible. The first was the vision-language connector: we expected \llava{}'s unpooled frozen-CLIP projection to keep the image strong enough to resist text in both directions. \llavanext{} shares that design and shows the asymmetry, so the connector is not the discriminating factor. The second was the visual token budget, which a within-model resolution sweep and a budget-matched cross-model pair both contradict (\S\ref{sec:models}). What remains is a bare observation: three of four models show the effect and one does not, and \llava{} is also the weakest of the four and the only one whose tokenizer splits every emotion label into multiple tokens. We flag those as candidates rather than explanations. Ruling out two architectural accounts is a more useful contribution here than proposing an untested third.

\paragraph{Conflicts with stored model knowledge.}
In conflicts between an image and knowledge stored in the model's weights, prior work finds that image tokens carry most of the causal effect \citep{nooralahzadeh2026, lietzow2026}. In our conflicts, the competing text is part of the prompt, and its effect travels through text-token states. Even the image signal becomes recoverable from text positions by the readout. Where a conflict is resolved therefore depends on the type of conflict, the layer, and the model design.

\paragraph{Implications.}
On the affect-sensitive task studied here, a single negative sentence overrides \qwen{}'s visual judgment on $76\%$ of conflict trials against $37\%$ in the mirror direction. The effect is not universal --- it is absent in \llava{} --- and its apparent size depends on the readout, so we would caution against treating any single figure as \emph{the} magnitude of negativity dominance in VLMs. For deployments where a caption or user message accompanies an image, the practical point is narrower and firmer: text and image are not interchangeable evidence, and which one wins depends on the valence of the text and on the model. For \gemma{}-like models, downstream text-token states are a concrete place to monitor, since they carry essentially all of the context difference and can be steered to change the output.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Mechanism.} Patching shows where the context difference travels, but not why negative context has the larger effect. We have not separately patched neutral-to-positive and neutral-to-negative changes. Near-zero recovery from image tokens is also partly expected because the image appears before the context. We have not tested the reverse order. Finally, the tokens before the answer may be a general place where prompt information gathers; a matched non-affective control is needed to test whether their role is specific to valence. The full-bank patching sentences differ in token length, so we also plan to repeat the experiment with the token-matched pairs. Under fixed negative context, the image-driven gap becomes too small to localize its path reliably.

\textbf{Statistics.} Our intervals are clustered over the 121 distinct photographs, and we additionally report crossed intervals that resample photographs and context sentences jointly (Table~\ref{tab:models}). The crossed intervals are the honest test of generalization to new sentences, and on the full bank, with six unrelated events per polarity, they are weak: no model clears zero on all three readouts there. The token-matched bank (\S\ref{sec:minimal}) addresses this directly --- removing event variance brings both the override gap and the mirror contrast to crossed intervals excluding zero, and all six pairs reproduce the effect individually. What remains unestablished is generalization beyond this \emph{style} of context sentence: every sentence in both banks follows the frame ``This photo was taken \ldots''. Widening the banks across sentence forms, not just adding more of the same frame, is the highest-value follow-up and requires no new method. One image appears in both polarity groups; removing it changes the contrast by $0.018$. All runs use a single seed.

\textbf{Measurement.} Behavioral valence is bounded between $-1$ and $1$, and models use that range very differently: \qwen{} saturates it while \gemma{} sits near the ceiling on positive images. We therefore report an override rate and an unbounded log-odds margin alongside it, and we show that the three readouts do not agree about which models show the effect most strongly (\S\ref{sec:models}). We regard that disagreement as a finding about the measure rather than noise. EMOTIC labels people within images, whereas we provide the whole image without marking the target person; a rerun with marked or cropped people is the direct fix. Two subsections were measured on an inference stack we later found to compute a different multimodal forward pass, and are withdrawn rather than reported (\S\ref{sec:prompts}). Every other number in the paper was measured on the reference implementation.

\textbf{Scope.} We study one dataset, one task family (person-emotion labeling), one context style, and four model designs. The mechanism analysis is detailed for \gemma{} and coarse for \qwen{}. This is a real mismatch: \gemma{} carries our most detailed mechanism results while showing the weakest behavioral asymmetry of the three positive models, because the frozen text-trained probe the mechanism work depends on was fit on its activations. Replicating the full patching analysis on \qwen{} would require refitting that probe and is the natural next step. Our claims therefore concern controlled image-context emotion judgments in the tested models. Other conflict domains, tasks, and architectures remain open.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Cross-modal conflict resolution is valence-asymmetric in three of the four VLM designs we test: negative language changes image-grounded emotion judgments more than positive language does. Holding the event fixed and flipping one valence word sharpens rather than removes it, and all six such pairs agree, so it tracks valence rather than event content. It is absent in \llava{}, whose two conflict directions are statistically indistinguishable. We report three readouts per model and find that they disagree about which model shows the effect most strongly, in a way that tracks how each model uses the bounded valence scale --- so the presence of the asymmetry is robust across measures while its magnitude is not. Two architectural explanations for the cross-model variation, the vision-language connector and the visual token budget, are tested and rejected; we leave the variation unexplained rather than substitute a third. In \gemma{}, patching shows that downstream text-token states carry essentially all of the context effect while image tokens carry none, and that the image's own valence becomes readable from those same states over depth. A direction learned only from text can then be used at those states to causally shift the output during conflict. Multimodal conflict therefore depends not only on which modalities disagree, but on the valence of their evidence, the architecture, and the scale on which the outcome is measured.

\subsection{Compositional emotion steering (supporting)}
In a 30-image pilot, combinations of text-derived appraisal directions produce specific emotions predicted by appraisal theory. Neither component alone produces anger: unpleasantness alone favors fear, while other-responsibility with its valence component removed favors joy. Combining the two at matched norm makes anger the top emotion on $97\%$ of images. This matches the appraisal prediction \emph{anger = negative + other-caused}. The pilot is supporting evidence that the appraisal directions have meaningful structure; it is not required for the conflict claims.

\section{Context banks, prompt, and label set}
\label{app:contexts}

\paragraph{Label set.}
The 13 allowed emotion labels are anger, boredom, disgust, fear, guilt, joy, pride, relief, sadness, shame, surprise, trust, and neutral. Behavioral valence is the total probability of \{joy, pride, relief, trust\} minus the total probability of \{anger, boredom, disgust, fear, guilt, sadness, shame\}. Surprise and neutral contribute to neither side. This partition matches the released code.

\section{Pilot vs.\ full bank}
\label{app:pilot}

A 40-image pilot used one context sentence per polarity. It suggested that the image dominated (ratio $0.15$--$0.22$) and that the valence asymmetry was very large. Both conclusions depended on one weak positive sentence. With the full context bank, the image and text effects become comparable ($0.86$--$1.19$), and the steering slope changes from $+0.350$ to $+0.215$. This pilot shows why conflict experiments should not rely on a single context sentence.

\section{Patching methodology}
\label{app:patching}

\paragraph{Same-image design (\S\ref{sec:patching}).}
For each image, the positive-context run supplies activations and the negative-context run receives them. We overwrite \texttt{resid\_post} across layers 13--17 for one aligned token group at a time. At the layer-18 probe, recovery is $(\text{patched} - \text{negative})/(\text{positive} - \text{negative})$. The groups are the 256 image tokens, question tokens, the beginning-of-sequence token, user-turn prefix markers, and the assistant-turn suffix (\texttt{<end\_of\_turn>\textbackslash n<start\_of\_turn>model}). The final scoring token is excluded from every group.

We use 60 images for each of two sentence pairs: championship/funeral and wonderful/devastating. Behavioral-valence recovery follows the same ordering as probe recovery but is noisier; all-text recovery is $73$--$78\%$. The full-bank sentences differ in token length, so matched groups occur at different absolute positions. A planned rerun with token-matched pairs removes this positional confound.

\paragraph{Cross-image design (\S\ref{sec:crosspatch}).}
The positive-image run supplies activations and the negative-image run receives them, while the context stays fixed. Their \texttt{input\_ids} are identical, so every text position can be matched exactly. We use 60 image pairs and patch three layer ranges: 0--12, 13--17, and 18--28. Behavioral valence is the main recovery measure. The layer-18 probe provides a check where valid; because its activation is computed from layer 17, it cannot measure recovery caused by patching layers 18--28. Patching every position except the scoring token recovers $100\%$, $100\%$, and $90\%$ across the three ranges. Under negative context, the image-driven gap shrinks to $0.08$, making the late-range denominator nearly zero. We report those confidence intervals but do not interpret them.

\paragraph{Qwen port.}
For Qwen, we use raw HuggingFace hooks and behavioral-valence recovery on 60 positive images across a broad middle-to-late layer range. Question tokens recover $12\%$, the turn markers recover $6\%$, and all aligned text positions together recover $65\%$. The combined recovery exceeds the sum of individual groups, consistent with the signal being distributed redundantly across text positions.

\section{Compute}
\label{app:compute}

All experiments use inference-only forward passes in bf16 on one A100-class GPU with 40 GB of memory. They use no gradients and no generation. Each model's full-bank base run is 150 person-annotations $\times$ 15 conditions $=$ 2{,}250 forward passes, and the \qwen{} minimal-pair run is another 2{,}250; four models are run, each with a 15-pass text-only control. The conflict steering sweep is 150 incongruent cells $\times$ 7 steering strengths $=$ 1{,}050 passes. Layerwise localization is 60 positive-group images $\times$ 14 contexts $=$ 840 passes, each cached at every layer in a single forward. Same-image patching covers 60 images $\times$ 2 sentence pairs $\times$ (7 token groups $+$ 2 baselines); cross-image patching covers 60 image pairs $\times$ 3 layer bands $\times$ (6 token groups $+$ 2 baselines). The \qwen{} resolution sweep repeats the base run at three image resolutions. Counts here cover the experiments reported; the two withdrawn subsections (\S\ref{sec:prompts}) consumed a further 13{,}500 and 4{,}500 passes that we do not report results from. \todo{Add wall-clock totals per run from the session logs for camera-ready; the full project including preliminary and failed runs used more compute than the reported experiments.}
