\begin{abstract}
When an image and its accompanying text disagree, a vision-language model (VLM) must decide which to follow. Prior work asks \emph{which modality} wins such conflicts; we ask whether the answer depends on \emph{what the text says}. We pair emotionally positive and negative photographs of people with short context sentences of the opposite valence, and measure how far each cue moves the model's emotion judgment relative to a neutral-context baseline. In \qwen{}, conflict resolution is asymmetric: measured against each image's own neutral-sentence baseline, a negative sentence overrides a positive image on $57\%$ of trials while a positive sentence overrides a negative image on $35\%$, a gap of $+21.7\%$ (uncorrected, $76\%$ versus $37\%$). Holding the event fixed and flipping a single valence word --- won$\leftrightarrow$lost, wonderful$\leftrightarrow$devastating --- sharpens the effect rather than removing it, and all six such pairs agree, with negative framing moving the judgment four to five times as far as its positive counterpart. The asymmetry therefore tracks valence, not event content. We evaluate four VLMs on three readouts --- a categorical override rate, a bounded graded score, and an unbounded log-odds margin --- and find the asymmetry is present in three of them at closely similar corrected magnitudes ($+21.7\%$, $+20.5\%$, $+18.1\%$) despite an eight-fold spread in image-token budget, though \emph{which readout registers it most strongly still varies by model}, tracking how each model uses the bounded scale. It is absent on \llava{}, whose two conflict directions are statistically indistinguishable. We report image-clustered and crossed (image $\times$ sentence) confidence intervals throughout; with six sentences of each valence the design establishes robustness to image sampling but not to sentence sampling, and we say so. Activation patching in \gemma{} shows the context effect is carried by text-token states, which recover $88$--$93\%$ of it, while the image's own valence is recoverable from image tokens early in the network and from text states later. At those states, a valence direction learned purely from text shifts image-conditioned output as strongly under conflict as without it. Multimodal conflict thus depends on the valence of the evidence, the architecture, and the scale on which the outcome is measured.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Show a vision-language model a smiling face alongside the sentence \emph{``This was taken at her best friend's funeral,''} and it must decide which signal to follow. Prior work shows that this choice varies with the task, model, prompt, relevance of the text, and relation between the two modalities \citep{deng2025blindfaith, mixedsignals2025, contextvqa2026, signpost2026}. One question remains open: under otherwise matched conditions, does positive text pull the model as strongly as negative text?

Human affective science suggests it should not. Negativity dominance --- the finding that mixtures of negative and positive cues are judged more negatively than their parts predict --- is among the most replicated results in psychology \citep{rozin2001, baumeister2001}, and context sentences are known to reshape how people read facial emotion \citep{aviezer2008, aviezer2012}. Whether VLMs show an analogous asymmetry, in which models, and through what computation, has not been established.

Our paradigm is simple. We take photographs of people from the extremes of the EMOTIC valence distribution \citep{kosti2019} and pair each with one-sentence contexts --- positive, negative, and neutral --- inserted into a fixed prompt that asks what the person is feeling. Both conflict directions are run (negative sentence on a positive image, and the mirror), and every effect is measured against the neutral-context baseline. We read the model's judgment two ways: behaviorally, from the probabilities it assigns to emotion words; and internally, with a linear valence probe trained only on text \citep{tak2025} and applied, frozen, under image input.

Five findings emerge.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Conflict resolution is valence-asymmetric (\qwen{}).} Against each image's own neutral-sentence baseline, a negative sentence overrides a positive image on $57\%$ of trials while a positive sentence overrides a negative image on $35\%$ (gap $+21.7\%$, 95\% CI clustered over images $[+10.9, +31.9]$; crossed over images \emph{and} sentences $[-8.6, +48.0]$). Uncorrected the figures are $76\%$, $37\%$ and $+39.4\%$; the correction matters and we explain it in \S\ref{sec:asymmetry}. \qwen{} is also the only model of the four that weights the two cues comparably ($|\betatxt|/|\betaimg| = 1.14$), so its asymmetry cannot be attributed to it ignoring the image.
  \item \textbf{It tracks valence, not event content.} Six token-matched minimal pairs --- identical sentences differing in one valence word --- \emph{sharpen} every metric relative to the varied set (baseline-corrected override gap $+23.1\%$, uncorrected $+41\%$; mirror contrast $+0.478$). The within-item contrast, holding photograph and event fixed, is $+1.148$ (CI $[+0.94, +1.34]$, $p < 0.001$), and all six pairs point the same way at ratios of $4.3$ to $5.5$. On the matched set the mirror contrast clears zero even when sentences are resampled alongside photographs. The baseline-corrected override gap does not ($[-2.6,+45.4]$); only its uncorrected version does, and we report the corrected one.
  \item \textbf{Three of four models agree on the size of the asymmetry once it is baselined; the fourth reverses.} We report a categorical override rate, a bounded graded score, and an unbounded log-odds margin for every model (Table~\ref{tab:models}). All three agree on \llava{}, where the effect is absent. On the other three they disagree in a way that tracks how each model uses the bounded scale: \qwen{} saturates it, so its log-odds score understates the effect, while \gemma{} sits near the ceiling on positive images, so its valence score does. Baselining the override gap against each image's own neutral-sentence rate --- the correction the graded readouts already apply --- brings the three positive models to $+21.7\%$, $+20.5\%$ and $+18.1\%$, within $3.6$ points of one another, and removes the appearance that \qwen{} is far the largest. The crossed bootstrap on the varied set is severe, and the models do not fare alike under it: \llavanext{} clears zero on all three readouts, \gemma{} on two, and \qwen{} --- our primary behavioral model --- on none. \qwen{}'s support under sentence resampling comes from the matched set (\S\ref{sec:minimal}), not from the varied one. We state that ordering rather than selecting a readout per model.
  \item \textbf{Two architectural explanations are ruled out.} The asymmetry is \emph{not} a property of the vision-language connector: \llavanext{} uses the same linear-projector family as \llava{} and does show it (corrected gap $+20.5\%$; mirror contrast $+0.413$, crossed CI $[+0.23,+0.59]$). It is also not driven by the visual token budget: across a seventeen-fold range in image tokens --- a resolution sweep on \qwen{} with identical weights ($128$ to $262$ tokens) and \llavanext{} at $2{,}147$ --- every baseline-corrected gap falls between $+18\%$ and $+26\%$. We report the variation across models as unexplained rather than substituting a third architectural story.
  \item \textbf{Both cues reach the same downstream text states (\gemma{}).} Activation patching shows the context effect is carried by downstream text-token states, which recover $88$--$93\%$ of it. The image's own valence is recoverable from image tokens early in the network ($100\%$ at layers 0--12) and increasingly from text positions later ($63\%$ at layers 18--28). A valence direction learned purely from text, injected at these states, shifts image-conditioned output as strongly under conflict as without it (slope $+0.335$ vs.\ $+0.336$).
\end{enumerate}

Together, these experiments establish the behavioral asymmetry in the model that shows it most cleanly, map how it varies across architectures and readouts, rule out two explanations for that variation, and trace where the competing signals travel inside \gemma{}. Two limits are load-bearing and we state them up front: with six sentences of each valence the design establishes robustness to \emph{image} sampling but not to \emph{sentence} sampling (\S\ref{sec:models}), and the mechanism experiments are run on \gemma{}, whose own behavioral asymmetry is the weakest of the three positive models (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

We scope our claims carefully. We do not claim to discover that text can override vision \citep{deng2025blindfaith, contextvqa2026}, nor the first mechanistic study of multimodal conflict \citep{seeingoverrides2026}, nor the first study of multimodal emotion conflict \citep{mosear2025}. And while the behavioral pattern is consistent with negativity dominance in humans, we treat it as an analogue, not the same construct.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Modality conflict in VLMs.}
Several lines of work create mismatched image-text pairs and ask which modality the model follows. \citet{deng2025blindfaith} show that VLMs disproportionately trust text when it has been corrupted, while \citet{mixedsignals2025} find that the preferred modality varies with task complexity and model. Contradictory persuasive text can override clear visual evidence across 11 VLMs \citep{contextvqa2026}, including in geolocation \citep{signpost2026}. Misleading instructions can also become \emph{more} influential when an image is present \citep{camel2025}. These studies show that image-text conflict is real and can matter for safety. They examine factors such as relevance, task difficulty, and the relation between the cues, but do not isolate whether matched positive and negative text exert different pull. We test that question in both conflict directions and across model designs.

\paragraph{Different kinds of conflict.}
A complementary line of work studies conflicts between visual input and knowledge stored in the model's weights. \citet{nooralahzadeh2026} find that image tokens carry nearly all of the effect when a model must override such prior knowledge; \citet{lietzow2026} identify a small set of late attention heads involved in that override; and \citet{seeingoverrides2026} steer the resulting decisions. Our setting differs because the competing text appears directly in the prompt. In \gemma{}, even the image's decision-relevant signal becomes recoverable from text positions over depth. The location of conflict resolution may therefore depend on the kind of conflict, the network depth, and the model design.

\paragraph{Emotion and affect in multimodal models.}
EMOTIC and related datasets are widely used to test VLM emotion recognition \citep{kosti2019, etesam2024, agarwal2026}. Other benchmarks study emotion conflicts between audio and vision \citep{mosear2025, emomm2026}. VEENA \citep{veena2026} identifies and intervenes on emotion-related circuits in large VLMs, but studies matching rather than conflicting cues and does not test valence asymmetry. In text-only LLMs, \citet{tak2025} locate appraisal-related information and steer emotion judgments, providing the method we build on. \citet{negbeforepos2026} report that positive and negative valence are processed differently across layers. We extend that question to behavioral conflicts between images and text.

\paragraph{Mechanistic interpretability of VLMs.}
Activation patching and causal tracing are established tools for locating information that affects a model's output \citep{meng2022, patchingbestpractices, attributionpatching}. Prior VLM studies show that image information moves into text or query positions in early-to-middle layers \citep{basu2024, whatsintheimage2025, narrowgate2024, towardsinterp2024}, and that middle-layer attention at the last token helps combine the modalities \citep{fcct2026}. Our cross-image result (\S\ref{sec:crosspatch}) agrees with this work. Our contribution is to trace the two signals during a conflict, not to establish image-to-text transfer itself. \citet{nikankin2025} find largely separate circuits for similar visual and textual tasks. That result is compatible with ours: different upstream computations can still feed information into the same downstream states. Other work finds that delimiter and assistant-header tokens can collect information from across a prompt \citep{xiao2024, activedormant2024, zhang2026anydepth}. In \gemma{} the context difference is carried by text-token states, but it is split about evenly between the question tokens and the tokens before the model's answer (\S\ref{sec:patching}), so our data do not single out the turn scaffold and we do not claim support for that account.

\paragraph{Human affective science.}
Negativity dominance \citep{rozin2001} and ``bad is stronger than good'' \citep{baumeister2001} are foundational asymmetries in human evaluation, and situational sentences modulate facial-affect perception \citep{aviezer2008, aviezer2012}. We use this literature as motivation; whether VLM valence asymmetries share anything computational with the human construct is an open question our operationalization (cross-modal override) does not settle.

\paragraph{Steering vectors.}
Difference-of-means steering can change model outputs, but that does not by itself reveal what computation changed \citep{steeringunreliability2025, steeringnonident2026}. We compare our valence direction with random directions of the same norm and with a direction for a different appraisal. We therefore claim only that steering changes the outcome under conflict, not that it changes how strongly the model weights each cue (\S\ref{sec:arbitration}).

\section{Experimental framework}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Task and stimuli.}
Each trial shows the model a photograph of a person and a one-sentence context. A fixed prompt asks, \emph{``What single emotion is this person feeling?''} (exact template in Appendix~\ref{app:contexts}). We use the 75 most positive and 75 most negative person annotations from the EMOTIC test split \citep{kosti2019}: 150 people across 121 distinct images.

We use two sets of context sentences, and refer to them throughout as the \emph{varied set} and the \emph{matched set}. The varied set is 6 positive, 6 negative and 2 neutral sentences describing different events --- ``This photo was taken moments after they won the championship,'' ``\ldots at the funeral of a close friend.'' The matched set is 6 pairs of sentences that describe the \emph{same} event and differ in exactly one word: won$\leftrightarrow$lost, wonderful$\leftrightarrow$devastating. Each pair is also the same length in tokens, so the position the model is scored at does not shift between them (Appendix~\ref{app:contexts}). The varied set asks whether the effect holds across different events; the matched set asks whether it is the valence word doing the work. Pairing every image with every sentence gives 15 conditions per image and 2{,}250 forward passes per set.

EMOTIC labels each person, but we give the model the whole image without a bounding box. We therefore checked whether the same image could contribute people to both valence groups. This occurs for exactly one of the 121 images. Excluding it barely changes the headline contrast ($+0.330 \to +0.312$ on the matched set). Reruns with the target person marked remain future work (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Design decisions.}
Two choices matter for interpreting the results. First, we measure every effect against a \emph{neutral-context} baseline rather than a prompt with no context. Adding any framing sentence, even a neutral one, changes the model's judgment (Appendix~\ref{app:nocontext}). The neutral baseline keeps the presence of a sentence fixed while its polarity changes. Second, we use sets of several contexts rather than a single positive and negative sentence. An early single-sentence pilot produced conclusions that did not survive when several sentences were used (Appendix~\ref{app:pilot}).

\paragraph{Models.}
We evaluate four VLMs spanning distinct vision-language designs. \qwen{} (native dynamic-resolution ViT) is our primary behavioral model: it is the only one of the four that weights the two cues comparably, so its conflict results are not confounded by it under-using one modality. \gemma{} (34 layers, $d_{\text{model}}{=}2560$; SigLIP vision tower pooled to 256 image tokens) is our primary \emph{mechanism} model, because the frozen text-trained probe of \S\ref{sec:foundations} was fit on its activations. \llava{} (frozen CLIP ViT through a linear projector, unpooled patch tokens) and \llavanext{} (the same projector family at a much larger token budget) complete the comparison. All models are run in bf16 through the standard HuggingFace implementation, with no intermediate wrapper. All measurements are single forward passes; nothing is generated. Compute details are in Appendix~\ref{app:compute}.

\paragraph{How we measure the model's answer.}
We never sample text. We read the model's judgment from the probabilities it assigns to 13 emotion words at the last prompt token (\emph{joy}, \emph{sadness}, \emph{fear}, and the rest listed in Appendix~\ref{app:contexts}), and summarize those probabilities three ways. Each is defined here and used consistently for the rest of the paper.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Override rate.} The fraction of conflict trials on which the single most probable emotion word matches the \emph{text}'s valence rather than the \emph{image}'s. Categorical, so it is comparable across models that use a graded scale differently.
  \item \textbf{Valence score.} $P(\text{positive words}) - P(\text{negative words})$, a graded number in $[-1, 1]$. Sensitive to how far a cue moves the judgment, but compressed near its ends.
  \item \textbf{Log-odds score.} $\log P(\text{positive words}) / P(\text{negative words})$. The same comparison without the bounds, so a shift near the extremes is not flattened.
\end{itemize}

We report all three throughout rather than choosing one, because they do not always agree and the disagreement is informative (\S\ref{sec:models}). Two derived quantities recur, and we name them here since they are our own shorthand rather than standard terms. The \textbf{mirror contrast} is how much further a negative sentence pulls a positive image than a positive sentence pulls a negative image --- the asymmetry itself, in whichever units the underlying score uses. The \textbf{within-item contrast} is the same comparison holding the photograph and the described event fixed, so that only the valence word differs (\S\ref{sec:minimal}).

\paragraph{Internal readout.}
Alongside behavior, we read the model's internal state with a linear \emph{pleasantness} probe. Linear probes are a standard tool for testing what a representation makes linearly available \citep{alain2016}; we follow \citet{tak2025}'s appraisal-probe setup specifically, training a ridge regression on text-only activations from crowd-enVENT \citep{troiano2023} at the last token of the layer-18 attention output. We then freeze the probe and apply it to image inputs without re-fitting it. Its performance under image input therefore tests whether a text-learned readout transfers across modalities. The probe tracks EMOTIC ground-truth valence at $\rho = 0.510$ (\S\ref{sec:foundations}). We call the feature it reads \emph{valence-sensitive}, rather than claiming it is the model's only valence representation. Reading and steering need different objects: the probe direction can read valence but does not move it, whereas the difference between the mean activation of high- and low-valence examples does. That contrast --- a linear direction estimated as a difference of class means and then added back into the residual stream --- is the standard construction in the steering literature \citep{turner2023, zou2023, marks2023, li2023iti, rimsky2024, arditi2024}. We adopt it unchanged; our contribution is to estimate the direction on \emph{text} and apply it under \emph{image} input, not the technique itself.

\section{Reading and steering valence across modalities}
\label{sec:foundations}

Before studying conflicts, we establish a cross-modal valence readout and show that it can affect behavior. Full details are in Appendix~\ref{app:foundations}.

\textbf{First, a text-trained probe locates valence-related information.} Following \citet{tak2025}, ridge probes for six appraisal dimensions peak in the middle of the network (layers 17--21 of 34) at the attention output, far above shuffled baselines. Pleasantness is strongest, with validation $r^2 = 0.641$ at layer 18. We therefore fix layer 18 as the readout and steering site for the remaining experiments.

\textbf{Second, the probe transfers to images.} Applied unchanged to image-conditioned activations, it tracks EMOTIC valence at Spearman $\rho = +0.510$ ($n{=}7{,}280$ images scored; polarity AUC $0.912$ on the 440 single-label polar images) and beats all 100 norm-matched random directions ($p{=}0.010$). After accounting for neutral and detailed captions, the image readout still explains unique variation in valence (semipartial $\rho = +0.201$, $p{<}0.001$). Thus, ordinary captioning does not fully explain the transferred signal.

\textbf{Third, a text-derived direction changes image-conditioned behavior.} Following the difference-of-means construction described above \citep{turner2023, marks2023, arditi2024}, we estimate the mean activation difference between high- and low-pleasantness text examples and add that direction into the residual stream at layer 18 under image input. Behavioral valence moves monotonically with the intervention (slope $+0.336$), about $9.6\times$ the norm-matched random control ($-0.035$) and $4.3\times$ the non-valence specificity control ($-0.078$). Together, these results support a valence-sensitive activation space that both modalities can influence. They do not imply that vision and language use the same upstream computation \citep{nikankin2025}.

\section{Valence-asymmetric conflict resolution}
\label{sec:conflict}

\subsection{Both cues matter, but not equally}
\label{sec:integration}

Before asking which cue wins, we check that both cues affect the model at all. Regressing the valence score on image valence and context valence, with both predictors standardized and using only trials that contain a sentence, gives positive weights for both in all four models: none of them ignores a modality, so the conflicts below are genuine competitions rather than one cue being unread.

The models differ in balance, and the difference matters for interpretation. \qwen{} weights the text slightly above the image ($|\betatxt|/|\betaimg| = 1.14$); the other three are image-led, between $0.44$ and $0.55$ (last row of Table~\ref{tab:models}). An override asymmetry is harder to read in an image-led model, because the text starts from a weaker position. \qwen{} is therefore our primary model for the behavioral results: whatever asymmetry it shows cannot be explained by it under-using the image. This balance is a property of the affective task studied here, not a general claim about these models.

\subsection{The asymmetry, in all four conditions}
\label{sec:asymmetry}

Table~\ref{tab:factorial} reports every combination of image and context valence for \qwen{}. Our primary comparison is between the two conflict conditions. A negative sentence moves positive-image judgments by $-1.156$. The reverse conflict, a positive sentence on negative images, moves judgments by $+0.762$. We call the difference between these magnitudes the \emph{mirror contrast}. Computed per image against that image's own neutral baseline and then averaged, it is $+0.409$ (bootstrap 95\% CI clustered over images $[+0.230, +0.588]$). Taking it instead as the difference of the cell means in Table~\ref{tab:factorial} gives $+0.394$; we report the per-image form throughout because it is the quantity the confidence intervals are computed on.

Negative images start closer to the bottom of the valence scale, so they have less room to move; the asymmetry could in principle be that geometry rather than an effect of valence. The log-odds score removes the bounds entirely, and the asymmetry does not vanish under it (\S\ref{sec:models}). Appendix~\ref{app:headroom} reports a second, cruder correction that divides each shift by the room remaining in its direction, and reaches the same conclusion. The categorical result agrees. We baseline it the same way: each image's override rate is measured against that image's own rate under a neutral sentence, because Appendix~\ref{app:nocontext} shows that any sentence shifts the readout, and \qwen{} in particular already reads a positive image as negative on $20\%$ of neutral-sentence trials while reading a negative image as positive on only $1.3\%$. Uncorrected, that asymmetry in the starting point is credited to the conflict. Corrected, negative context overrides a positive image on $57\%$ of trials and positive context overrides a negative image on $35\%$, a gap of $+21.7\%$ (95\% CI clustered over images $[+10.9, +31.9]$). The uncorrected figures are $76\%$, $37\%$ and $+39.4\%$ ($[+29.5, +49.0]$); we report both throughout and rely on the corrected one.

Figure~\ref{fig:conflict} shows this pattern against \llava{}, where the same two conflict directions are symmetric. A second comparison uses only positive images. On these, negative and positive contexts differ by $3.64\times$ in their distance from neutral ($-1.156$ vs.\ $+0.317$). This ratio compares a conflicting context with an \emph{agreeing} context on the same image; it is not the mirror contrast, and we report it separately. On its own it can mislead: \llava{} reaches $3.05\times$ purely through the score's bounds even though its mirror contrast is null (\S\ref{sec:models}). We therefore never use this ratio as evidence on its own.

\paragraph{Which sources of variation the intervals cover.}
All intervals in this paper are percentile bootstrap intervals with $2{,}000$ resamples at a fixed seed; what changes between them is the unit resampled. The confidence intervals above are clustered over photographs, which treats the six sentences of each valence as a fixed set. Treating stimulus items as fixed when one intends to generalize over them is a long-recognized error --- \citet{clark1973} named it the language-as-fixed-effect fallacy, and it remains the standard argument for modeling items and subjects as crossed random effects \citep{baayen2008, westfall2014}. Our contexts are exactly such items: we want claims about negative \emph{sentences}, not about these six. Resampling sentences as well (\S\ref{sec:models}, Table~\ref{tab:models}) widens \qwen{}'s mirror-contrast interval to $[-0.16, +0.91]$, which includes zero; its baseline-corrected override gap also includes zero ($[-8.6, +48.0]$), though the uncorrected version does not ($[+6.4, +66.5]$). With six sentences of each valence the design is well powered for generalization across images and weakly powered for generalization across sentences, because resampling six \emph{unrelated events} perturbs the estimate heavily. The matched set of \S\ref{sec:minimal} removes that event variance and recovers a crossed interval excluding zero on both the override gap and the mirror contrast. We report both intervals everywhere rather than choosing the narrower one.

\subsection{Is it the valence, or the event? Matched sentence pairs}
\label{sec:minimal}

Could the asymmetry come from the sentences rather than their valence? Without an image, the positive and negative sentences have similar mean strength, but with six sentences of each valence a non-significant test cannot establish that the sets are equivalent. A sharper concern remains: ``funeral'' and ``championship'' describe different events, and some events may point more directly to particular emotion labels.

We therefore built six \emph{minimal pairs}. Each pair uses the same sentence and changes only one valence-bearing word: won$\leftrightarrow$lost, best$\leftrightarrow$worst, got$\leftrightarrow$lost, wonderful$\leftrightarrow$devastating, celebration$\leftrightarrow$memorial, or joyful$\leftrightarrow$heartbreaking. Each pair also has the same token count under the model's tokenizer, so the readout position does not change between the two members. Because the two members share a context id, the design supports a strict \emph{within-item} test: for a fixed image and a fixed event, compare the negative member's effect against the positive member's, each measured from that image's own neutral baseline, $|\Delta_{\rm neg}| - |\Delta_{\rm pos}|$.

The asymmetry survives, and sharpens (Table~\ref{tab:minimal}). The corrected override gap is $+23.1\%$ against $+21.7\%$ for the varied set, the mirror contrast rises from $+0.409$ to $+0.496$, and the model moves from balanced to text-led ($|\betatxt|/|\betaimg| = 1.97$). Since the matched set removes event variation, the effect is not carried by ``funeral'' and ``championship'' denoting different kinds of situations. Nor is it carried by the sentences themselves: run with no image, the six pairs are balanced (pooled ratio $1.02$, mean paired difference $+0.33$ log-odds, $p = 0.69$), while with an image the same six are unanimous and about thirty times larger in paired terms. Appendix~\ref{app:contexts} gives the control in full, including the one model that does \emph{not} pass it.

The more consequential gain is in the crossed intervals. On the varied set the mirror contrast does not survive sentence resampling ($[-0.16, +0.91]$); on the matched set it does ($[+0.11, +0.83]$). The baseline-corrected override gap tightens too but still includes zero ($[-2.6, +45.4]$), so the mirror contrast is the readout that carries this result; the uncorrected override gap would clear zero here ($[+16.7, +63.2]$) and we do not lean on it. Removing between-sentence event variance is what buys this: the six minimal sentences differ only in valence, so resampling them perturbs the estimate far less than resampling six unrelated events does. The unbounded margin remains negative and straddles zero on both sets ($-1.13$ here), which is the saturation property discussed in \S\ref{sec:models} rather than a failure of this control.

\paragraph{The strictest test.}
The within-item contrast is the tightest form of the claim: same photograph, same event, one word changed. On positive images it is $+1.148$ (bootstrap CI over images $[+0.943, +1.344]$; Wilcoxon $p < 0.001$; $n = 62$ images $\times$ 6 pairs).

\section{Mechanism: where the two signals travel}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\subsection{Which token positions carry the sentence's effect?}
\label{sec:patching}

Before asking \emph{where} the context signal is carried, we located \emph{when} it appears. Projecting the last-token state onto the frozen probe direction at every layer, the gap between positive- and negative-context runs first clears the noise of the early layers around layer 12 of 34, and is already a large, consistent effect there (Appendix~\ref{app:layerwise}). We therefore intervene just downstream of that, over layers 13--17.

Which token positions carry the context's valence signal? We hold the image constant and compare a positive-context run with a negative-context run. For one group of token positions at a time, we replace the negative-context activations over layers 13--17 with the corresponding activations from the positive-context run. We then measure how much the readout moves toward the positive-context value. The final token whose state we score is excluded from every patched group, so the experiment does not recover the answer by directly replacing that state. The result repeats across two context pairs (Table~\ref{tab:patching}); Appendix~\ref{app:patching} gives the full method.

The context difference is carried by downstream text-token states: patching all aligned text recovers $88$--$93\%$ of it. Within the text stream the effect is \emph{distributed} rather than concentrated in one group, but the two pairs differ and the intervals show it. On pair 1 the question tokens and the tokens marking the start of the model's answer contribute indistinguishably ($47.3\%$ $[43.4, 51.0]$ vs.\ $46.4\%$ $[44.4, 48.3]$) and their contributions are additive: they sum to $93.7\%$, inside the interval for patching all aligned text at once ($[91.5, 94.7]$). On pair 2 neither holds --- the question tokens dominate ($54.3\%$ $[51.9, 57.0]$ vs.\ $39.8\%$ $[37.3, 42.1]$, non-overlapping) and the groups are sub-additive, summing to $94.1\%$ against a joint recovery of $88.3\%$ $[87.2, 89.4]$. What survives across both pairs is the weaker claim: neither group alone accounts for the effect, so we do not attribute it to the turn scaffold specifically.

The zeros in the lower block of Table~\ref{tab:patching} are not evidence about where visual information lives. Image tokens, BOS and the user-turn prefix all precede the context in the prompt, and the image is held constant across the two runs, so under causal masking those states are identical and the patch copies a value onto itself. We confirmed this directly: on one pair all $60$ rows are bitwise unchanged, and on the other the largest deviation is $5\times10^{-2}$ against a difference of $1.3$, i.e.\ forward-pass noise. The rows are reported because a nonzero value there would indicate a bug in the position alignment. The question of where the image's own valence is represented requires varying the image, which is what \S\ref{sec:crosspatch} does. Three limits matter.

\textbf{First,} this experiment locates the positive-versus-negative difference; it does not explain why negative context has the larger behavioral effect. A symmetric model could show the same pattern of information flow. \textbf{Second,} the tokens before the answer may generally collect information from the prompt. Without a matched non-affective control, we cannot tell whether their role is specific to valence. \textbf{Third,} the $7$--$12\%$ not recovered by all aligned text is simply the remainder under this intervention. It is not an estimate of the context tokens' causal share, because patching effects need not add linearly. The varied-set sentences also differ in token length, placing aligned groups at different absolute positions. A planned rerun on the token-matched pairs removes this last confound.

\subsection{Where does the image's own valence live?}
\label{sec:crosspatch}

We next ask where the \emph{image's own} valence can be recovered. We hold the context fixed and neutral, then compare a positive-image run with a negative-image run. Because the text inputs are identical, every text position can be matched exactly. Table~\ref{tab:crosspatch} reports how much of the image-driven difference is recovered when activations are patched over three layer ranges. Behavioral valence is the main readout; the layer-18 probe agrees in the ranges where it is valid.

Early in the network, the image-driven difference is recovered almost entirely from image tokens ($100.1\%$ $[99.6, 100.5]$) and essentially not at all from text positions ($1.7\%$ $[-0.6, 4.6]$). Late in the network the ordering reverses, and the intervals separate cleanly: image-token recovery falls to $31.1\%$ $[24.1, 38.4]$ while text-position recovery rises to $63.4\%$ $[55.1, 71.5]$. We interpret this as visual valence becoming \emph{additionally} readable from text-token states over depth, rather than migrating out of the image tokens --- a third of it is still recoverable there at layers 18--28. This agrees with prior evidence of image-to-text transfer \citep{basu2024, whatsintheimage2025} and corrects the idea that visual valence remains confined to image tokens.

In the mid band, where both readouts are valid, they disagree about which group dominates: the layer-18 probe gives image $74.9\%$ $[71.3, 78.9]$ vs.\ text $87.4\%$ $[85.9, 88.8]$, while behavioral valence gives image $96.0\%$ $[92.1, 99.6]$ vs.\ text $74.1\%$ $[66.5, 81.6]$. Both orderings are supported by non-overlapping intervals, in opposite directions. Both come from the same model and inference stack, so this reflects \emph{where} the readout is taken --- at layer 18 versus after sixteen further layers of mixing --- not a measurement error. We report both and draw no conclusion about mid-band ordering.

The same downstream text states can therefore carry information about both the context and the image. This provides a concrete site where the two cues can compete. It does not show whether visual information continues to move into text states while negative context is winning. In a confirmatory run with fixed negative context, the image-driven valence gap shrinks from $0.87$ to $0.08$. That small denominator makes late-layer recovery unstable, so we could not answer the question. Mid-layer image-token recovery remains high at $85\%$ (CI $[51, 150]$), but the wide interval prevents a stronger conclusion.

\section{Generality across models, and causal control}
\label{sec:robustness}

\subsection{Four vision-language designs, three readouts}
\label{sec:models}

Is the asymmetry a property of VLMs in general, or of particular designs? We run the same conflict experiment on four ways of connecting vision to language. \qwen{} uses a native vision transformer accepting varying resolutions. \gemma{} pools its SigLIP output to 256 image tokens. \llava{} passes unpooled patches from a frozen CLIP tower through a linear projection. \llavanext{} uses that same linear-projector design at a far larger token budget (2{,}147 image tokens).

Because each model uses the bounded valence scale differently, no single readout is neutral. We therefore report three: the categorical \emph{override rate} (\S\ref{sec:setup}), the bounded \emph{mirror contrast}, and an \emph{unbounded log-odds margin}, $\log P(\text{pos})/P(\text{neg})$, which removes the scale's bounds entirely. For each we give two intervals: one that resamples photographs only, and one that resamples photographs \emph{and} context sentences together, so that the sentences are treated as a sample rather than as fixed \citep{clark1973, westfall2014}. The second is the stricter test and the one that licenses claims about negative language in general (Table~\ref{tab:models}).

Three results follow.

\textbf{Three models agree on the size of the asymmetry; the fourth is null.} \qwen{}, \llavanext{} and \gemma{} agree on the \emph{size} of the effect on both continuous measures: their mirror contrasts are $+0.409$, $+0.413$ and $+0.405$, and their baseline-corrected override gaps are $+21.7\%$, $+20.5\%$ and $+18.1\%$. Two readouts, one graded and one categorical, place three unrelated vision-language designs within a few points of each other.

They do not fare alike under the crossed bootstrap, and the ordering does not favour us: on the varied set \llavanext{} clears zero on all three readouts, \gemma{} on two (mirror contrast and unbounded margin), and \qwen{} on none. \qwen{}'s only varied-set crossed interval excluding zero is the \emph{uncorrected} override gap, which we do not rely on; its support under sentence resampling comes from the matched set of \S\ref{sec:minimal}. So the model that survives the strictest test on every readout is \llavanext{} --- which is also the one model that fails the stimulus control of Appendix~\ref{app:contexts}. We report both facts about it in the same breath rather than citing whichever is convenient.

This agreement is visible only after correction. Uncorrected, the same three gaps read $+39.4\%$, $+18.7\%$ and $+12.2\%$ --- a three-fold spread that invites an architectural explanation --- and the spread is an artifact of where each model starts. \qwen{} already reads a positive image as negative on $20\%$ of neutral-sentence trials and a negative image as positive on $1.3\%$, a twenty-point head start for the direction we are measuring; \gemma{} is skewed the other way ($2.7\%$ versus $7.3\%$), which suppressed its gap below significance. Correcting each cell against its own neutral baseline moves \qwen{} down and \gemma{} up, and \gemma{}'s photo-clustered interval clears zero once corrected ($[+7.5,+28.7]$) where uncorrected it did not ($[-0.4,+23.7]$). We flag that the correction was applied after seeing the uncorrected numbers; it is not a post-hoc choice of estimator but the baselining rule \S\ref{sec:asymmetry} already states for the graded readouts, which the categorical one had simply escaped.

\llava{} clears zero on no readout: its mirror contrast is $+0.016$ and its unbounded margin $+0.05$, both sitting essentially on zero. We describe \llava{} as a null rather than a reversal. Its negative override gap ($-10.0\%$ corrected) does not survive sentence resampling, and its two conflict directions move the judgment equally far once the room available in each direction is taken into account (Appendix~\ref{app:headroom}), so the apparent reversal is scale geometry.

\textbf{The readouts disagree, and the disagreements are systematic.} \llavanext{} is the only model that clears zero on all three. \qwen{} clears on none of the three we rely on: its bounded valence saturates at $\pm 1$, which compresses both graded scores, and its corrected override gap does not survive sentence resampling on the varied set. \gemma{} shows a third pattern --- its unbounded margin is the largest of any model ($+4.64$ log-odds) and its mirror contrast clears, while its override gap does not, because its positive-image baseline sits near the ceiling so a large log-odds move rarely crosses a category boundary. This is why we report all three rather than selecting one per model. It also means the size of the effect is not readout-independent, and we make no claim about its magnitude in absolute terms.

\textbf{Two architectural explanations are ruled out.} The first is the connector. \llavanext{} shares \llava{}'s frozen-CLIP linear-projector design and \emph{does} show the asymmetry (mirror contrast $+0.413$, crossed $[+0.23,+0.59]$), so ``linear-projector designs resist textual override'' is false, and we retract that claim. One caveat attaches to \llavanext{} specifically: it is the only model whose context sentences are imbalanced when run with no image (raw log-odds ratio $1.69$, unsaturated; Appendix~\ref{app:contexts}), so part of its asymmetry may be inherited from the stimuli rather than produced by the conflict. \qwen{} and the matched set pass the same control ($0.94$ and $1.02$), which is why we do not rest the connector argument on \llavanext{} alone. The second is the visual token budget. A resolution sweep on \qwen{} with identical weights is flat across a two-fold change in image tokens (corrected gaps --- 128 tokens: $+25.8\%$; 262 tokens: $+20.6\%$ and $+20.9\%$; all intervals overlapping, image discriminability held at AUC $0.983$--$0.986$). A budget-matched cross-model pair now \emph{agrees} rather than diverging (\gemma{} at 256 tokens, $+18.1\%$; \qwen{} at 262 tokens, $+21.7\%$), and \llavanext{} shows the effect at $2{,}147$ image tokens ($+20.5\%$). Across a seventeen-fold range in image-token budget and three model families, every corrected gap lies between $+18\%$ and $+26\%$. Budget fails to predict the asymmetry not because it predicts the wrong direction but because, once the readout is baselined, there is almost nothing left for it to predict. We note that on the uncorrected numbers this same comparison appeared to separate by 27 points in the direction opposite to the hypothesis; that separation was a baseline artifact.

We therefore report the variation across models as \emph{unexplained}. Two candidate accounts have been tested and rejected; we prefer to say so rather than substitute a third. A caveat on \llavanext{}: it changes the language backbone (Mistral) relative to \llava{} (Vicuna), so backbone and token budget are not fully separated. The clean comparison, \texttt{llava-v1.6-vicuna-7b}, has not been run (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Mechanism replication.} A coarse version of the patching result also replicates on \qwen{}: all text positions together recover $65\%$ of the context difference. (Image tokens again recover none, and again this is forced by prompt order rather than measured.) \qwen{} spreads the information redundantly --- individual token groups recover little (question $12\%$, turn markers $6\%$) while their union recovers far more than their sum, which is not the case in \gemma{}, where the groups are close to additive (\S\ref{sec:patching}). We did not run mechanism experiments on \llava{}, where there is no asymmetry to explain.

\subsection{A text-derived direction still moves the answer under conflict}
\label{sec:arbitration}

Finally, we test whether the activation space of \S\ref{sec:foundations} can causally change a judgment during conflict. On 150 conflict trials, injecting the text-derived pleasantness direction at layer 18 moves behavioral valence with a slope of $+0.335$ per unit of steering strength. The no-conflict slope measured the same way is $+0.336$ (\S\ref{sec:foundations}), so \emph{conflict does not attenuate the intervention at all}: a direction learned only from text is as effective at moving the output when an image contradicts the sentence as when nothing does. Before running the sweep we verified the injected direction against its published norm at this site (relative error $0.14\%$), since a wrong residual site does not error --- it steers with a vector that means nothing.

The frozen probe readout is flat under this intervention (slope $+0.000$). That is expected rather than a null result: the probe tap sits inside layer 18, upstream of the residual-stream injection at the same layer, so it is invariant to steering by construction. We report it to show the injection is acting where we claim.

This establishes causal control of the outcome, but not of the competition between the cues: a general output-valence direction could shift the final score without changing either cue's weight. Testing whether steering changes override rates or cue coefficients is the natural next step.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Where the cues meet.}
The patching experiments point to the same downstream text-token states for both cues. Same-image patching shows that these states carry the context difference. Cross-image patching shows that visual valence becomes recoverable from them over depth. These findings identify a plausible meeting point for image-text conflict. They do not show why negative context has the larger effect. The pair-level results offer one clue: negative framing is consistent, while positive framing depends more on the event. Patching neutral-to-positive and neutral-to-negative changes separately would test whether the asymmetry arises while information is being carried through the network or is already present in the inputs.

\paragraph{Why does \llava{} differ?}
We do not know, and we have ruled out the two explanations we found most plausible. The first was the vision-language connector: we expected \llava{}'s unpooled frozen-CLIP projection to keep the image strong enough to resist text in both directions. \llavanext{} shares that design and shows the asymmetry, so the connector is not the discriminating factor. The second was the visual token budget, which a within-model resolution sweep and a budget-matched cross-model pair both contradict (\S\ref{sec:models}). What remains is a bare observation: three of four models show the effect and one does not, and \llava{} is also the weakest of the four and the only one whose tokenizer splits every emotion label into multiple tokens. We flag those as candidates rather than explanations. Ruling out two architectural accounts is a more useful contribution here than proposing an untested third.

\paragraph{Conflicts with stored model knowledge.}
In conflicts between an image and knowledge stored in the model's weights, prior work finds that image tokens carry most of the causal effect \citep{nooralahzadeh2026, lietzow2026}. In our conflicts, the competing text is part of the prompt, and its effect travels through text-token states. Even the image signal becomes recoverable from text positions by the readout. Where a conflict is resolved therefore depends on the type of conflict, the layer, and the model design.

\paragraph{Implications.}
On the affect-sensitive task studied here, a single negative sentence overrides \qwen{}'s visual judgment on $57\%$ of conflict trials against $35\%$ in the mirror direction, once each image's neutral-sentence baseline is subtracted. Uncorrected the same comparison reads $76\%$ against $37\%$, and the difference between those two framings is a caution in itself: the effect is not universal --- it is absent in \llava{} --- and its apparent size depends on both the readout and the baseline, so we would caution against treating any single figure as \emph{the} magnitude of negativity dominance in VLMs. For deployments where a caption or user message accompanies an image, the practical point is narrower and firmer: text and image are not interchangeable evidence, and which one wins depends on the valence of the text and on the model. For \gemma{}-like models, downstream text-token states are a concrete place to monitor, since they carry essentially all of the context difference and can be steered to change the output.

\paragraph{Broader impacts and responsible use.}
Inferring emotion from photographs of people is a contested application, and our use of it is diagnostic: we treat the model's emotion judgment as an instrument for studying how it arbitrates between modalities, not as evidence that such judgments are valid. Nothing here should be read as support for deploying affect inference on images of people, and the asymmetry we report is a reason for more caution rather than a capability to build on. Three risks follow from the results specifically. \textbf{First,} a system that reads images for affect can have its judgment moved by one accompanying sentence, so any pipeline accepting user-supplied text alongside images inherits a cheap and directional lever --- and because the asymmetry favors negative framing, the cheap direction is the harmful one. \textbf{Second,} the localization that supplies a monitoring site also supplies a steering handle: a direction fit on text alone shifts image-conditioned output under conflict (\S\ref{sec:models}). We report the site because the vulnerability exists whether or not it is documented, and because defenders gain more from knowing where to look than an attacker gains from learning that a direction is linear. \textbf{Third,} the resulting error is not evenly distributed. If negative framing moves judgments further than positive framing does, the people most often described in negative terms absorb most of it, and we did not measure whether the effect varies across the demographic groups EMOTIC contains. That measurement is a prerequisite for any deployment claim, and we make none. The photographs are of real people, used under EMOTIC's research access terms; we release index files and context sentences, never images.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Mechanism.} Patching shows where the context difference travels, but not why negative context has the larger effect. We have not separately patched neutral-to-positive and neutral-to-negative changes. The zero recovery from image tokens in the same-image experiment is an arithmetic consequence of prompt order --- the image precedes the context, so those states cannot differ between the two runs --- and says nothing about where visual content is represented. Placing the context before the image would turn that row into a real measurement; we have not run it. Finally, the tokens before the answer may be a general place where prompt information gathers; a matched non-affective control is needed to test whether their role is specific to valence. The varied-set patching sentences differ in token length, so we also plan to repeat the experiment with the token-matched pairs. Under fixed negative context, the image-driven gap becomes too small to localize its path reliably.

\textbf{Statistics.} Our intervals are clustered over the 121 distinct photographs, and we additionally report crossed intervals that resample photographs and context sentences jointly (Table~\ref{tab:models}). The crossed intervals are the honest test of generalization to new sentences, and on the varied set, with six unrelated events of each valence, they are weak, and unevenly so: only \llavanext{} clears zero on all three readouts there, \gemma{} clears on two, and \qwen{}, our primary behavioral model, on none. The matched set (\S\ref{sec:minimal}) addresses this for \qwen{} --- removing event variance brings the mirror contrast to a crossed interval excluding zero ($[+0.11, +0.83]$), and all six pairs reproduce the effect individually. The baseline-corrected override gap does not clear zero there either ($[-2.6, +45.4]$), so on the matched set it is the mirror contrast that carries the claim. What remains unestablished is generalization beyond this \emph{style} of context sentence: every sentence in both sets follows the frame ``This photo was taken \ldots''. Widening the sets across sentence forms, not just adding more of the same frame, is the highest-value follow-up and requires no new method. One image appears in both polarity groups; removing it changes the contrast by $0.018$. All runs use a single seed.

\textbf{Measurement.} Behavioral valence is bounded between $-1$ and $1$, and models use that range very differently: \qwen{} saturates it while \gemma{} sits near the ceiling on positive images. We therefore report an override rate and an unbounded log-odds margin alongside it, and we show that the three readouts do not agree about which models show the effect most strongly (\S\ref{sec:models}). We regard that disagreement as a finding about the measure rather than noise. EMOTIC labels people within images, whereas we provide the whole image without marking the target person; a rerun with marked or cropped people is the direct fix. Every number in this paper was measured through the models' standard implementations. Two further experiments --- a six-way rephrasing sweep of the question and a within-family scale comparison --- were run earlier through a wrapper library we subsequently found to compute a different multimodal forward pass from the reference implementation, and we do not report them. Both bore on absolute override rates, the quantity that discrepancy most affects. Question phrasing is therefore untested here, and is the most obvious control still missing (\S\ref{sec:conclusion}).

\textbf{Scope.} We study one dataset, one task family (person-emotion labeling), one context style, and four model designs. The mechanism analysis is detailed for \gemma{} and coarse for \qwen{}. This is a mismatch, though a smaller one than the uncorrected numbers suggested: \gemma{} carries our most detailed mechanism results while showing the weakest behavioral asymmetry of the three positive models, because the frozen text-trained probe the mechanism work depends on was fit on its activations. Once the override gap is baselined the three sit within $3.6$ points of each other (\S\ref{sec:models}), so the model we understand best is no longer the outlier it appeared to be. Replicating the full patching analysis on \qwen{} would require refitting that probe and is the natural next step. Our claims therefore concern controlled image-context emotion judgments in the tested models. Other conflict domains, tasks, and architectures remain open.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Cross-modal conflict resolution is valence-asymmetric in three of the four VLM designs we test: negative language changes image-grounded emotion judgments more than positive language does. Holding the event fixed and flipping one valence word sharpens rather than removes it, and all six such pairs agree, so it tracks valence rather than event content. It is absent in \llava{}, whose two conflict directions are statistically indistinguishable. We report three readouts per model. Once the categorical readout is baselined the way the graded ones already are, the three affected models agree closely on the size of the effect ($+21.7\%$, $+20.5\%$, $+18.1\%$), and the apparent three-fold spread between them turns out to reflect where each model starts rather than how it resolves conflict. The readouts still disagree about which model shows the effect most strongly, in a way that tracks how each uses the bounded valence scale. Two architectural explanations for the residual cross-model variation, the vision-language connector and the visual token budget, are tested and rejected --- the second because, corrected, there is little variation left for it to explain. In \gemma{}, patching shows that downstream text-token states carry essentially all of the context effect ($88$--$93\%$), and that the image's own valence becomes readable from those same states over depth. A direction learned only from text, injected at those states, shifts the output during conflict as strongly as it does with no conflict present. Multimodal conflict therefore depends not only on which modalities disagree, but on the valence of their evidence, the architecture, and the scale on which the outcome is measured.

\subsection{Combining directions (supporting evidence)}
In a 30-image pilot, combinations of text-derived appraisal directions produce specific emotions predicted by appraisal theory. Neither component alone produces anger: unpleasantness alone favors fear, while other-responsibility with its valence component removed favors joy. Combining the two at matched norm makes anger the top emotion on $97\%$ of images. This matches the appraisal prediction \emph{anger = negative + other-caused}. The pilot is supporting evidence that the appraisal directions have meaningful structure; it is not required for the conflict claims.

\paragraph{Are the sentences balanced in isolation?}
The asymmetry could be a property of the sentences rather than of the conflict: if the negative members are simply the stronger stimuli, they would dominate whatever the image showed. We test this directly by running every sentence with no image at all.

On \qwen{} the bounded valence readout cannot answer the question. With no image the model reads even a neutral sentence as sadness ($-0.996$), so $87\%$ of matched-set rows sit on a scale bound and the ratio comes out at exactly $1.00$ by construction --- a number produced by two saturated constants, not by the sentences. We therefore read this control on the unbounded log-odds margin (\S\ref{sec:models}), which has no bounds to pin against.

On that scale the six matched pairs are balanced. The pooled ratio is $|\text{neg}|/|\text{pos}| = 1.02$; per pair it ranges from $0.67$ to $1.22$ with four of six leaning negative, and the mean paired difference is $+0.33$ log-odds (Wilcoxon $p = 0.69$, $n = 6$ pairs). The varied set gives $0.94$, leaning marginally the other way. With an image present the same six pairs are unanimous and roughly thirty times larger in paired terms ($+10.46$ log-odds, six of six, Wilcoxon $p = 0.031$). The asymmetry therefore appears only when an image is present; it is not carried into the conflict by the sentences.

Three caveats. Six pairs give little power, so this bounds a gross imbalance rather than excluding a small one. The control is per-model, and \llavanext{} is \emph{not} balanced in isolation (raw ratio $1.69$, with no saturation to excuse it), so its behavioral result carries a stimulus confound the other models do not --- we flag this in \S\ref{sec:models} rather than treating the control as settled for all four. Finally, every model's no-image baseline sits below the centre of the scale (\qwen{} $-8.19$ in log-odds, \llavanext{} $-1.41$, \llava{} $-0.64$, \gemma{} $-0.33$): with no image these models lean toward sadness, which depresses any ratio taken against the neutral sentence, so the ratios above are taken against zero.

The earlier \gemma{} figures of $1.06$ (varied) and $1.25$ (matched) were measured on the superseded inference stack and on the bounded readout. They reproduce exactly from those files and we record them as provenance, but they do not bear on the numbers above.

\section{Where in depth the context signal appears}
\label{app:layerwise}

Where in the network do positive- and negative-context runs begin to differ? At each layer we project the last-token state onto the frozen probe direction --- reading a fixed direction off every depth, in the manner of the logit lens and its successors \citep{nostalgebraist2020, belrose2023}, as a diagnostic rather than a re-fitted probe. We summarize the gap between the two context polarities as a paired effect size over images, $d(L)$, which removes the growth in residual-stream norm with depth.

The separation first clears the pre-divergence noise floor at layer 12 of 34, and it arrives already large: $|d| = 3.9$ there, against a floor set at three times the median absolute separation over the first eight layers. It does not then amplify --- $|d|$ is $2.0$ at the readout layer 18 and $3.0$ at layer 28, with no late peak. In \emph{raw} projection units the separation does grow by more than an order of magnitude over the same span ($-0.39$ at layer 12 to $-6.27$ at layer 28), but that growth tracks the residual-stream norm rather than the consistency of the effect, which is why we report the scale-free form. We therefore locate the entry of the context signal in the mid band and make no claim that it strengthens with depth. This shows where the difference becomes legible to this probe; it does not show where the signal is created.

The context's effect on the final token need not be direct: the final token could read the context words itself, or the context could first reshape other token positions that the final token then reads. The next experiment settles this causally.

\section{Correcting for the scale's bounds}
\label{app:headroom}

The valence score is bounded in $[-1,1]$, and negative images start near its floor, so a negative sentence has less room to push them than a positive sentence has to pull them up. Two corrections are possible. The principled one is to drop the bounds: the log-odds score of \S\ref{sec:setup} does this, and is what the main text relies on. The cruder one, reported here for completeness, divides each shift by the room remaining in the direction it moves.

Under that correction the asymmetry persists in \qwen{} (drop $0.714$ vs.\ rise $0.394$ on the varied set; $0.924$ vs.\ $0.518$ on the matched set) and vanishes in \llava{} (drop $0.329$ vs.\ rise $0.332$), matching what the log-odds score shows. We prefer the log-odds score in the main text because dividing by remaining room is unstable when the denominator is small: \gemma{}'s positive-image baseline sits at $+0.905$, close enough to the ceiling that the ratio becomes meaningless. We do not report a head-room-corrected number for \gemma{} for that reason.

\section{Pilot vs.\ varied set}
\label{app:pilot}

A 40-image pilot used one context sentence of each valence. It suggested that the image dominated (ratio $0.15$--$0.22$) and that the valence asymmetry was very large. With the full sentence set, the image and text effects become comparable ($0.86$--$1.19$), and the steering slope changes from $+0.350$ to $+0.215$. \section{Patching methodology}
\label{app:patching}

\paragraph{Same-image design (\S\ref{sec:patching}).}
For each image, the positive-context run supplies activations and the negative-context run receives them. We overwrite \texttt{resid\_post} across layers 13--17 for one aligned token group at a time. At the layer-18 probe, recovery is $(\text{patched} - \text{negative})/(\text{positive} - \text{negative})$. The groups are the 256 image tokens, question tokens, the beginning-of-sequence token, user-turn prefix markers, and the assistant-turn suffix (\texttt{<end\_of\_turn>\textbackslash n<start\_of\_turn>model}). The final scoring token is excluded from every group. The first, third and fourth of these groups lie before the context, and the image is identical in the two runs, so their donor and recipient states coincide and their recovery is $0\%$ by construction rather than by measurement; they serve as a check on the position alignment.

We use 60 images for each of two sentence pairs: championship/funeral and wonderful/devastating. Recovery uses the same ratio-of-sums estimator as the cross-image design, bootstrapped over the 60 images with $2{,}000$ resamples; \texttt{src/experiments/patching\_intervals.py} recomputes every interval in Tables~\ref{tab:patching} and~\ref{tab:crosspatch} from the saved per-row outputs. Behavioral-valence recovery follows the same ordering as probe recovery but is noisier; all-text recovery is $73$--$78\%$. The full-bank sentences differ in token length, so matched groups occur at different absolute positions. A planned rerun with token-matched pairs removes this positional confound.

\paragraph{Cross-image design (\S\ref{sec:crosspatch}).}
The positive-image run supplies activations and the negative-image run receives them, while the context stays fixed. Their \texttt{input\_ids} are identical, so every text position can be matched exactly. We use 60 image pairs and patch three layer ranges: 0--12, 13--17, and 18--28. Recovery is a ratio of sums, $\sum(\text{patched} - \text{neg}) / \sum(\text{pos} - \text{neg})$, rather than a mean of per-row ratios, because an individual pair's denominator can sit near zero; its interval is a percentile bootstrap over the 60 pairs with $2{,}000$ resamples. Behavioral valence is the main recovery measure. The layer-18 probe provides a check where valid; because its activation is computed from layer 17, it cannot measure recovery caused by patching layers 18--28. Patching every position except the scoring token recovers $99.9\%$, $100.0\%$, and $89.9\%$ across the three ranges. Under negative context, the image-driven gap shrinks to $0.08$, making the late-range denominator nearly zero. We report those confidence intervals but do not interpret them.

\paragraph{Qwen port.}
For Qwen, we use raw HuggingFace hooks and behavioral-valence recovery on 60 positive images across a broad middle-to-late layer range. Question tokens recover $12\%$, the turn markers recover $6\%$, and all aligned text positions together recover $65\%$. The combined recovery exceeds the sum of individual groups, consistent with the signal being distributed redundantly across text positions.

\section{Compute}
\label{app:compute}

All experiments use inference-only forward passes in bf16 on one A100-class GPU with 40 GB of memory. They use no gradients and no generation. Each model's full-bank base run is 150 person-annotations $\times$ 15 conditions $=$ 2{,}250 forward passes, and the \qwen{} minimal-pair run is another 2{,}250; four models are run, each with a 15-pass text-only control. The conflict steering sweep is 150 conflict trials $\times$ 7 steering strengths $=$ 1{,}050 passes. Layerwise localization is 60 positive-group images $\times$ 14 contexts $=$ 840 passes, each cached at every layer in a single forward. Same-image patching covers 60 images $\times$ 2 sentence pairs $\times$ (7 token groups $+$ 2 baselines); cross-image patching covers 60 image pairs $\times$ 3 layer bands $\times$ (6 token groups $+$ 2 baselines). The \qwen{} resolution sweep repeats the base run at three image resolutions. Counts here cover the experiments reported; the two withdrawn subsections consumed a further 13{,}500 and 4{,}500 passes that we do not report results from. \todo{Add wall-clock totals per run from the session logs for camera-ready; the full project including preliminary and failed runs used more compute than the reported experiments.}
