\begin{abstract}
When an image and its accompanying text disagree, a vision-language model (VLM) must decide which to follow. Prior work asks \emph{which modality} wins such conflicts; we ask whether the answer depends on \emph{what the text says}. We pair photographs of people from the extremes of the EMOTIC valence distribution with one-sentence contexts of the opposite valence and measure how far each cue moves the model's emotion judgment from its neutral-context baseline. Our sharpest test holds the photograph and event fixed and flips one valence word --- won$\leftrightarrow$lost, wonderful$\leftrightarrow$devastating. Across six such pairs, negative framing moves \qwen{}'s judgment four to five times as far as positive framing; all six agree, and the within-item contrast is $+1.148$ (photo-clustered CI $[+0.94,+1.34]$). With no image, the same six pairs show no detectable mean difference (pooled ratio $1.02$, $p=0.69$), which argues against a large wording imbalance but cannot exclude a small one. On a varied set of six unrelated events per valence the effect is less precise: a negative sentence overrides a positive image on $57\%$ of trials against $35\%$ in the mirror direction (gap $+21.7\%$, $[+10.9,+31.9]$ over photographs), but resampling the six events as well widens the interval through zero. In \gemma{}, replacing all downstream text-position states in a negative-context run with those from a positive-context run restores $88$--$93\%$ of the context difference. Replacing states across different images shows that the visual difference is restored mainly from image positions early in the network and from text positions later. A valence direction estimated only from text still moves the image-conditioned answer during conflict. Across four VLMs, however, the measured result depends on both the summary score and how split emotion words are scored: evaluating only the first piece of a word creates an apparent null in one model and reverses the categorical ordering of another. We therefore make no architectural generalization.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Show a vision-language model a smiling face alongside the sentence \emph{``This was taken at her best friend's funeral,''} and it must decide which signal to follow. Prior work shows that this choice varies with the task, model, prompt, relevance of the text, and relation between the two modalities \citep{deng2025blindfaith, mixedsignals2025, contextvqa2026, signpost2026}. One question remains open: under otherwise matched conditions, does positive text pull the model as strongly as negative text?

Human affective science suggests it should not. Negativity dominance --- the finding that mixtures of negative and positive cues are judged more negatively than their parts predict --- is among the most replicated results in psychology \citep{rozin2001, baumeister2001}, and context sentences are known to reshape how people read facial emotion \citep{aviezer2008, aviezer2012}. Whether VLMs show an analogous asymmetry, in which models, and through what computation, has not been established.

Our paradigm is simple. We take photographs of people from the extremes of the EMOTIC valence distribution \citep{kosti2019} and pair each with one-sentence contexts --- positive, negative, and neutral --- inserted into a fixed prompt that asks what the person is feeling. Both conflict directions are run (negative sentence on a positive image, and the mirror), and every effect is measured against the neutral-context baseline. We read the model's judgment two ways: behaviorally, from the probabilities it assigns to emotion words; and internally, with a linear valence probe trained only on text \citep{tak2025} and applied, frozen, under image input.

Four findings emerge.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{One-word flips give the cleanest result.} Six token-matched pairs differ in a single valence word --- won$\leftrightarrow$lost, wonderful$\leftrightarrow$devastating --- with the photograph and event held fixed. Negative framing moves \qwen{}'s judgment four to five times as far as positive framing, all six pairs agree, and the within-item contrast is $+1.148$ (photo-clustered CI $[+0.94,+1.34]$). With no image, the same pairs show no detectable mean difference ($+0.33$ log-odds, Wilcoxon $p=0.69$ over six pairs); with an image the difference is $+10.46$ and all six point the same way ($p=0.031$ over pairs). This control argues against a large wording imbalance, although six pairs cannot exclude a small one.
  \item \textbf{The asymmetry also appears across a varied sentence set, less sharply.} On six unrelated events of each valence, a negative sentence overrides a positive image on $57\%$ of \qwen{} trials while a positive sentence overrides a negative image on $35\%$ (gap $+21.7\%$, 95\% CI clustered over images $[+10.9, +31.9]$; uncorrected $76\%$, $37\%$ and $+39.4\%$, corrected as \S\ref{sec:asymmetry} explains). Resampling those six events as well as the photographs widens the interval to include zero ($[-8.6, +48.0]$). This motivates the matched-pair test: with only six unrelated events, the varied set is precise across images but not across sentences. \qwen{} is also the only model of the four weighting both cues comparably ($|\betatxt|/|\betaimg| = 1.14$), so its asymmetry is not explained by ignoring the image.
  \item \textbf{In \gemma{}, internal replacement experiments locate states that can restore each cue's effect.} Replacing all downstream states at text positions with those from the opposite-context run restores $88$--$93\%$ of the context difference. In a separate cross-image intervention, image positions restore the visual difference early in the network ($100\%$ at layers 0--12), while text positions restore more of it later ($63\%$ at layers 18--28). A valence direction estimated only from text still moves the output while an image contradicts the sentence (slope $+0.335$). The corresponding no-conflict slope is $+0.336$, but the runs are not a matched equivalence test.
  \item \textbf{Measured strength depends heavily on the summary score and on how split labels are scored, and no architectural boundary survives.} We report three measures for every model (Table~\ref{tab:models}), and which clears zero under sentence resampling varies by model. The original analysis evaluated only the first content token of labels split into several tokens; scoring each complete emotion word turns \llava{} from a null into the model that clears all three crossed tests and reverses \llavanext{}'s categorical gap. Neither the way vision is connected to language nor the number of image tokens explains the remaining variation. We therefore make no architectural claim.
\end{enumerate}

The behavioral result is cleanest in one model when one word is changed and the photograph and event are held fixed. The no-image control argues against a large difference in sentence strength, but six pairs cannot exclude a smaller difference. The internal experiments then show where the two cues can be recovered in \gemma{}, while the four-model comparison sets the boundary of the claim: it does not support a general conclusion about model design. Two limits matter throughout. The varied set contains only six sentences of each valence and is therefore weakly powered across sentences (\S\ref{sec:models}); the detailed internal experiments use one model rather than all four (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

We scope our claims carefully. We do not claim to discover that text can override vision \citep{deng2025blindfaith, contextvqa2026}, nor the first mechanistic study of multimodal conflict \citep{seeingoverrides2026}, nor the first study of multimodal emotion conflict \citep{mosear2025}. And while the behavioral pattern is consistent with negativity dominance in humans, we treat it as an analogue, not the same construct.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Modality conflict in VLMs.}
Several lines of work create mismatched image-text pairs and ask which modality the model follows. \citet{deng2025blindfaith} show that VLMs disproportionately trust text when it has been corrupted, while \citet{mixedsignals2025} find that the preferred modality varies with task complexity and model. Contradictory persuasive text can override clear visual evidence across 11 VLMs \citep{contextvqa2026}, including in geolocation \citep{signpost2026}. Misleading instructions can also become \emph{more} influential when an image is present \citep{camel2025}. These studies show that image-text conflict is real and can matter for safety. They examine factors such as relevance, task difficulty, and the relation between the cues, but do not isolate whether matched positive and negative text exert different pull. We test that question in both conflict directions and across model designs.

\paragraph{Different kinds of conflict.}
A complementary line of work studies conflicts between visual input and knowledge stored in the model's weights. \citet{nooralahzadeh2026} find that image tokens carry nearly all of the effect when a model must override such prior knowledge; \citet{lietzow2026} identify a small set of late attention heads involved in that override; \citet{seeingoverrides2026} steer the resulting decisions; and \citet{unraveling2024} both detect such conflicts and mitigate them by suppressing logits from the less confident modality. Our setting differs because the competing text appears directly in the prompt. In \gemma{}, even the image's decision-relevant signal becomes recoverable from text positions over depth. The location of conflict resolution may therefore depend on the kind of conflict, the network depth, and the model design.

\paragraph{Emotion and affect in multimodal models.}
EMOTIC and related datasets are widely used to test VLM emotion recognition \citep{kosti2019, etesam2024, agarwal2026}. Other benchmarks study emotion conflicts between audio and vision \citep{mosear2025, emomm2026}. VEENA \citep{veena2026} identifies and intervenes on emotion-related circuits in large VLMs, but studies matching rather than conflicting cues and does not test valence asymmetry. In text-only LLMs, \citet{tak2025} locate appraisal-related information and steer emotion judgments, providing the method we build on. \citet{negbeforepos2026} report that positive and negative valence are processed differently across layers. We extend that question to behavioral conflicts between images and text.

\paragraph{Mechanistic interpretability of VLMs.}
Activation patching and causal tracing are established tools for testing which internal states are sufficient to restore an output difference \citep{meng2022, patchingbestpractices, attributionpatching}. Prior VLM studies show that image information becomes available from text or query positions in early-to-middle layers \citep{basu2024, whatsintheimage2025, narrowgate2024, towardsinterp2024}, and that middle-layer attention at the last token helps combine the modalities \citep{fcct2026}. Our contribution is to compare the states affected by each cue during conflict, not to establish image-to-text transfer itself. \citet{luo2025} show cross-modal transfer for \emph{task vectors}: a vector estimated from examples in one modality can trigger the corresponding task in another. That is the closest prior evidence for transferring an intervention vector across modalities, but it is not a demonstration of a valence direction. We instead ask whether a direction estimated from pleasant and unpleasant text examples can move an image-conditioned judgment when image and text disagree. \citet{nikankin2025} find largely separate circuits for similar visual and textual tasks, which remains compatible with different upstream computations affecting the same downstream states. \citet{conflictchallenges2025} find a linearly decodable \emph{conflict} signal in intermediate layers and present correlational evidence that conflict detection and resolution are separable. Our layerwise probe instead reads the sentence's valence, not conflict detection, and our replacement experiments test which states can restore an output difference; we do not equate the two signals. Other work finds that delimiter and assistant-header tokens can collect information from across a prompt \citep{xiao2024, activedormant2024, zhang2026anydepth}. In \gemma{} the context difference is split between the question tokens and the tokens before the model's answer (\S\ref{sec:patching}), so our data do not single out the turn scaffold.

\paragraph{Human affective science.}
Negativity dominance \citep{rozin2001} and ``bad is stronger than good'' \citep{baumeister2001} are foundational asymmetries in human evaluation, and situational sentences modulate facial-affect perception \citep{aviezer2008, aviezer2012}. We use this literature as motivation; whether VLM valence asymmetries share anything computational with the human construct is an open question our operationalization (cross-modal override) does not settle.

\paragraph{Steering vectors.}
Difference-of-means steering can change model outputs, but that does not by itself reveal what computation changed \citep{steeringunreliability2025, steeringnonident2026}. We compare our valence direction with random directions of the same norm and with a direction for a different appraisal. We therefore claim only that steering changes the outcome under conflict, not that it changes how strongly the model weights each cue (\S\ref{sec:arbitration}).

\section{Experimental framework}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Task and stimuli.}
Each trial shows the model a photograph of a person and a one-sentence context. The semantic prompt is fixed and asks, \emph{``What single emotion is this person feeling?''}; each model uses its native chat template (the Gemma form appears in Appendix~\ref{app:contexts}). We use the 75 most positive and 75 most negative person annotations from the EMOTIC test split \citep{kosti2019}: 150 people across 121 distinct images.

We use two sets of context sentences, and refer to them throughout as the \emph{varied set} and the \emph{matched set}. The varied set is 6 positive, 6 negative and 2 neutral sentences describing different events --- ``This photo was taken moments after they won the championship,'' ``\ldots at the funeral of a close friend.'' The matched set is 6 pairs of sentences that describe the \emph{same} event and differ in exactly one word: won$\leftrightarrow$lost, wonderful$\leftrightarrow$devastating. Each pair is also the same length in tokens, so the position the model is scored at does not shift between them (Appendix~\ref{app:contexts}). The varied set asks whether the effect holds across different events; the matched set asks whether it is the valence word doing the work. Pairing every image with every sentence gives 15 conditions per image and 2{,}250 forward passes per set.

EMOTIC labels each person, but we give the model the whole image without a bounding box. We therefore checked whether the same image could contribute people to both valence groups. This occurs for exactly one of the 121 images. Excluding it barely changes the headline contrast ($+0.496 \to +0.505$ on the matched set). Reruns with the target person marked remain future work (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Design decisions.}
Two choices matter for interpreting the results. First, we measure every effect against a \emph{neutral-context} baseline rather than a prompt with no context, so that the presence of a sentence is held fixed and only its polarity varies. A no-context prompt differs from every experimental condition in a way unrelated to valence; the neutral baseline removes that difference (Appendix~\ref{app:nocontext}). Second, we use sets of several contexts rather than a single positive and negative sentence. An early single-sentence pilot produced conclusions that did not survive when several sentences were used (Appendix~\ref{app:pilot}).

\paragraph{Models.}
We evaluate four VLMs spanning distinct vision-language designs. \qwen{} is our primary behavioral model because it is the only one of the four that gives image and text comparable weight on this task. \gemma{} (34 layers; SigLIP vision encoder pooled to 256 image positions) is our primary mechanism model because the text-trained probe of \S\ref{sec:foundations} was fit on its internal states. \llava{} and \llavanext{} both use a frozen CLIP vision encoder and a learned linear projection into the language model, but at different image-token budgets. All conflict runs and all reported image-conditioned behavioral scores use the models' standard HuggingFace implementations in bf16. The conflict measurements score single forward passes rather than generated answers. Earlier text-only probe training and caption-control activations used TransformerBridge; their relevant text path and activation site were subsequently checked against the reference implementation (\S\ref{sec:limitations}). Compute details are in Appendix~\ref{app:compute}.

\paragraph{How we measure the model's answer.}
We never sample an answer. Instead we score a fixed list of 13 emotion words (\emph{joy}, \emph{sadness}, \emph{fear}, and the rest, listed in Appendix~\ref{app:contexts}) and compare them only with one another. This defines the measurement target as the model's relative preference among those labels and avoids adding generation and string-matching choices. It does \emph{not} eliminate surface-form competition: as \citet{holtzman2021} show, an offered label can still lose probability to an unoffered synonym. Our results therefore concern this stated 13-label set.

Emotion words are not all one token. In \qwen{} and \gemma{} every label is a single token once the leading space is accounted for, but in both LLaVA models several are split --- \emph{boredom} and \emph{disgust} take three tokens each. We therefore score the whole word: each of its tokens in turn, conditioned on the prompt and on the label's own earlier tokens, with the log probabilities added. Scoring only the first token would compare the beginning of one word against the whole of another.

Adding the token scores is not the only option, and it is not the usual one --- averaging them instead, to normalize for length, is standard in multiple-choice evaluation \citep{brown2020}. Averaging fails here for a specific reason: our options are single words rather than sentences, so once the first token commits to an unusual word the remaining tokens are nearly certain, and the average rewards long labels almost mechanically. In \llava{} the averaged score correlates with label length at $r = 0.93$ and puts $2{,}246$ of $2{,}250$ trials on one of the two three-token words. Adding leaves a much smaller bias, $r = -0.23$, and it runs \emph{against} the effect we report: \llava{}'s negative labels are the longer ones ($1.9$ content tokens on average against $1.0$ for the positive labels), so adding penalizes exactly the labels that an override requires. We use the summed score throughout and report the averaged one as a sensitivity check (\S\ref{sec:limitations}). We then normalize the 13 scores into a distribution over that fixed list and summarize it three ways.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Override rate.} The fraction of conflict trials on which the single most probable emotion word matches the \emph{text}'s valence rather than the \emph{image}'s. Categorical, so it is comparable across models that use a graded scale differently.
  \item \textbf{Valence score.} $P(\text{positive words}) - P(\text{negative words})$, a graded number in $[-1, 1]$. Sensitive to how far a cue moves the judgment, but compressed near its ends.
  \item \textbf{Log-odds score.} $\log P(\text{positive words}) / P(\text{negative words})$. The same comparison without the bounds, so a shift near the extremes is not flattened.
\end{itemize}

We report all three throughout rather than choosing one, because they do not always agree and the disagreement is informative (\S\ref{sec:models}). Two derived quantities recur, and we name them here since they are our own shorthand rather than standard terms. The \textbf{mirror contrast} is how much further a negative sentence pulls a positive image than a positive sentence pulls a negative image --- the asymmetry itself, in whichever units the underlying score uses. The \textbf{within-item contrast} is the same comparison holding the photograph and the described event fixed, so that only the valence word differs (\S\ref{sec:minimal}).

\paragraph{Internal readout.}
Alongside behavior, we use a linear regression probe to predict \emph{pleasantness} from the model's internal state. Linear probes test whether information can be recovered by a simple linear map \citep{alain2016}. Following the broader recommendation to evaluate probes against control tasks \citep{hewitt2019}, we also report shuffled-label baselines. Following \citet{tak2025}, we train ridge regression on text-only states from crowd-enVENT \citep{troiano2023} at the last token of Gemma's layer-18 attention output. We then freeze it and apply it to image inputs without re-fitting. The probe tracks EMOTIC valence at $\rho=0.510$ (\S\ref{sec:foundations}). We call what it reads \emph{valence-sensitive} and do not claim it is the model's only valence representation. A separate direction is needed to change the model: we subtract the mean state for low-valence text examples from the mean for high-valence examples and add the result to the model's state. This difference-of-means construction is standard in steering work \citep{turner2023, zou2023, marks2023, li2023iti, rimsky2024, arditi2024}; we adopt it unchanged. Luo et al.'s task-vector result establishes a related form of cross-modal intervention transfer \citep{luo2025}. Our narrower addition is to estimate an affective direction from text and apply it while image and text disagree (\S\ref{sec:arbitration}).

\section{Reading and steering valence across modalities}
\label{sec:foundations}

Before studying conflicts, we establish a cross-modal valence readout and show that it can affect behavior. Full details are in Appendix~\ref{app:foundations}.

\textbf{First, a text-trained probe locates valence-related information.} Following \citet{tak2025}, ridge probes for six appraisal dimensions peak in the middle of the network (layers 17--21 of 34) at the attention output, far above shuffled-label baselines. Pleasantness reaches validation $r^2=0.641$ at layer 18, which we use only to select the fixed site for later experiments; it is not an untouched test-set estimate.

\textbf{Second, the probe transfers to images.} Applied unchanged to image-conditioned states, it tracks EMOTIC valence at Spearman $\rho=+0.510$ ($n=7{,}280$ images scored; polarity AUC $0.912$ on the 440 single-label polar images). It exceeds all 100 random directions of the same length (empirical $p=0.010$), showing that this result is unusual among the tested random directions. After accounting for both a neutral caption and a detailed perceptual caption of each image, the probe retains a smaller association with image valence (semipartial $\rho=+0.201$, $p<0.001$). Captioning therefore does not fully explain the association.

\textbf{Third, a text-derived direction changes image-conditioned behavior.} Following the difference-of-means construction described above \citep{turner2023, marks2023, arditi2024}, we estimate the mean activation difference between high- and low-pleasantness text examples and add that direction into the residual stream at layer 18 under image input. Behavioral valence moves monotonically with the intervention (slope $+0.336$), about $9.6\times$ the effect of a random direction of the same length ($-0.035$) and $4.3\times$ that of a direction for a different appraisal dimension ($-0.078$). Together, these results support a valence-sensitive activation space that both modalities can influence. They do not imply that vision and language use the same upstream computation \citep{nikankin2025}.

\section{Valence-asymmetric conflict resolution}
\label{sec:conflict}

\subsection{Both cues matter, but not equally}
\label{sec:integration}

Before asking which cue has more influence, we check that both affect the model at all. Regressing the valence score on image valence and context valence, with both predictors standardized and using only trials that contain a sentence, gives positive weights for both in all four models. Thus both cues measurably affect the answer rather than one being ignored.

The models differ in balance, and the difference matters for interpretation. \qwen{} weights the text slightly above the image ($|\betatxt|/|\betaimg| = 1.14$); the other three are image-led, between $0.44$ and $0.55$ (last row of Table~\ref{tab:models}). An override asymmetry is harder to read in an image-led model, because the text starts from a weaker position. \qwen{} is therefore our primary model for the behavioral results: whatever asymmetry it shows cannot be explained by it under-using the image. This balance is a property of the affective task studied here, not a general claim about these models.

\subsection{The asymmetry, in all four conditions}
\label{sec:asymmetry}

Table~\ref{tab:factorial} reports every combination of image and context valence for \qwen{}. Our primary comparison is between the two conflict conditions. A negative sentence moves positive-image judgments by $-1.156$. The reverse conflict, a positive sentence on negative images, moves judgments by $+0.762$. We call the difference between these magnitudes the \emph{mirror contrast}. Computed per image against that image's own neutral baseline and then averaged, it is $+0.409$ (bootstrap 95\% CI clustered over images $[+0.230, +0.588]$). Taking it instead as the difference of the cell means in Table~\ref{tab:factorial} gives $+0.394$; we report the per-image form throughout because it is the quantity the confidence intervals are computed on.

Because the valence score is bounded, changes near either end can be compressed. The log-odds score removes those bounds, and the conclusion does not depend on the bounded score alone (\S\ref{sec:models}). Appendix~\ref{app:headroom} reports a second, less stable check that divides each shift by the room remaining in its direction. We baseline the categorical measure in the same way as the graded measures: each image's override rate is measured against its own rate under a neutral sentence, which holds sentence presence fixed. This matters because \qwen{} already reads a positive image as negative on $20\%$ of neutral-sentence trials while reading a negative image as positive on only $1.3\%$. Without correction, that difference in starting rates is credited to the conflict. After correction, negative context overrides a positive image on $57\%$ of trials and positive context overrides a negative image on $35\%$, a gap of $+21.7\%$ (95\% CI clustered over images $[+10.9,+31.9]$). The uncorrected figures are $76\%$, $37\%$, and $+39.4\%$ ($[+29.5,+49.0]$); we report both and rely on the corrected one.

Figure~\ref{fig:conflict} shows the pattern in \qwen{} and in complete-label \llava{}. A second comparison uses only positive images. On these, \qwen{}'s negative and positive contexts differ by $3.64\times$ in their distance from neutral ($-1.156$ vs.\ $+0.317$). This ratio compares a conflicting context with an \emph{agreeing} context on the same image; it is not the mirror contrast. Because it also inherits the bounded scale's ceiling and floor, we report it only as a descriptive companion to the three primary readouts.

\paragraph{Which sources of variation the intervals cover.}
Every interval in this paper is a percentile bootstrap interval with $2{,}000$ resamples at a fixed seed. What changes between them is which thing gets resampled, and we report two. The one labelled \emph{photo-clustered} resamples photographs and treats the six sentences of each valence as fixed. The one labelled \emph{crossed} resamples photographs and sentences together, so it also covers the possibility that a different six sentences would have given a different answer.

Treating stimulus items as fixed when the intention is to generalize over them is a long-recognized error: \citet{clark1973} named it the language-as-fixed-effect fallacy, and it remains the standard argument for treating items and participants alike as sampled \citep{baayen2008, westfall2014}. Our sentences are exactly such items --- we want a claim about negative \emph{sentences}, not about these six. Resampling them as well (Table~\ref{tab:models}) widens \qwen{}'s mirror-contrast interval to $[-0.16, +0.91]$, which includes zero, and its baseline-corrected override gap to $[-8.6, +48.0]$, which also includes zero, though the uncorrected version does not ($[+6.4, +66.5]$). With six sentences of each valence the design is well powered across images and weakly powered across sentences, because resampling six \emph{unrelated events} perturbs the estimate heavily. The matched set of \S\ref{sec:minimal} removes that event variance and brings the mirror contrast to a crossed interval excluding zero ($[+0.11, +0.83]$); the baseline-corrected override gap does not clear zero even there ($[-2.6, +45.4]$). We report both intervals everywhere rather than choosing the narrower one.

\subsection{Is it the valence, or the event? Matched sentence pairs}
\label{sec:minimal}

Could the asymmetry come from the sentences rather than their valence? Without an image, the positive and negative sentences have similar mean strength, but with six sentences of each valence a non-significant test cannot establish that the sets are equivalent. A sharper concern remains: ``funeral'' and ``championship'' describe different events, and some events may point more directly to particular emotion labels.

We therefore built six \emph{minimal pairs}. Each pair uses the same sentence and changes only one valence-bearing word: won$\leftrightarrow$lost, best$\leftrightarrow$worst, got$\leftrightarrow$lost, wonderful$\leftrightarrow$devastating, celebration$\leftrightarrow$memorial, or joyful$\leftrightarrow$heartbreaking. Each pair also has the same token count under the model's tokenizer, so the readout position does not change between the two members. Because the two members share a context id, the design supports a strict \emph{within-item} test: for a fixed image and a fixed event, compare the negative member's effect against the positive member's, each measured from that image's own neutral baseline, $|\Delta_{\rm neg}| - |\Delta_{\rm pos}|$.

The estimates are larger in the matched set (Table~\ref{tab:minimal}). Removing between-sentence event variance is what buys this: the six minimal sentences differ only in valence, so resampling them perturbs the estimate far less than resampling six unrelated events does. The unbounded margin remains negative and straddles zero on both sets ($-1.13$ here), which is the saturation property discussed in \S\ref{sec:models} rather than a failure of this control.

\section{Mechanism: which internal states restore each cue?}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\subsection{Which token positions carry the sentence's effect?}
\label{sec:patching}

Before asking \emph{where} the context signal is carried, we located \emph{when} it appears. Projecting the last-token state onto the frozen probe direction at every layer, the gap between positive- and negative-context runs first clears the noise of the early layers around layer 12 of 34, and is already a large, consistent effect there (Appendix~\ref{app:layerwise}). We therefore intervene just downstream of that, over layers 13--17.

Which token positions carry the context's valence signal? We hold the image constant and compare a positive-context run with a negative-context run. For one group of token positions at a time, we replace the negative-context activations over layers 13--17 with the corresponding activations from the positive-context run. We then measure how much the readout moves toward the positive-context value. The final token whose state we score is excluded from every patched group, so the experiment does not recover the answer by directly replacing that state. The result repeats across two context pairs (Table~\ref{tab:patching}); Appendix~\ref{app:patching} gives the full method.

Replacing all aligned downstream text states restores $88$--$93\%$ of the context difference. Within those positions, no single group is sufficient, but the two sentence pairs differ. On pair 1 the question tokens and the tokens marking the start of the model's answer restore statistically indistinguishable shares ($47.3\%$ $[43.4, 51.0]$ vs.\ $46.4\%$ $[44.4, 48.3]$), and their sum lies inside the interval for replacing all aligned text at once. On pair 2 the question-token estimate is larger ($54.3\%$ $[51.9, 57.0]$ vs.\ $39.8\%$ $[37.3, 42.1]$), and the individual estimates sum to more than the joint recovery. We therefore make only the shared claim: neither group alone restores the full difference, and patching effects should not be interpreted as additive causal shares.

The zeros in the lower block of Table~\ref{tab:patching} are not evidence about where visual information lives. Image tokens, BOS and the user-turn prefix all precede the context in the prompt, and the image is held constant across the two runs, so under causal masking those states are identical and the patch copies a value onto itself. We confirmed this directly: on one pair all $60$ rows are bitwise unchanged, and on the other the largest deviation is $5\times10^{-2}$ against a difference of $1.3$, i.e.\ forward-pass noise. The rows are reported because a nonzero value there would indicate a bug in the position alignment. The question of where the image's own valence is represented requires varying the image, which is what \S\ref{sec:crosspatch} does. Three limits matter.

\textbf{First,} this experiment locates the positive-versus-negative difference; it does not explain why negative context has the larger behavioral effect. A symmetric model could show the same pattern of information flow. \textbf{Second,} the tokens before the answer may generally collect information from the prompt. Without a matched non-affective control, we cannot tell whether their role is specific to valence. \textbf{Third,} the $7$--$12\%$ not recovered by all aligned text is simply the remainder under this intervention. It is not an estimate of the context tokens' causal share, because patching effects need not add linearly. The varied-set sentences also differ in token length, placing aligned groups at different absolute positions. A planned rerun on the token-matched pairs removes this last confound.

\subsection{Where does the image's own valence live?}
\label{sec:crosspatch}

We next ask where the \emph{image's own} valence can be recovered. We hold the context fixed and neutral, then compare a positive-image run with a negative-image run. Because the text inputs are identical, every text position can be matched exactly. Table~\ref{tab:crosspatch} reports how much of the image-driven difference is recovered when activations are patched over three layer ranges. Behavioral valence is the main readout; the layer-18 probe agrees in the ranges where it is valid.

Early in the network, the image-driven difference is recovered almost entirely from image positions ($100.1\%$ $[99.6, 100.5]$) and essentially not at all from text positions ($1.7\%$ $[-0.6, 4.6]$). Late in the network the ordering reverses: image-position recovery falls to $31.1\%$ $[24.1, 38.4]$ while text-position recovery rises to $63.4\%$ $[55.1, 71.5]$. We interpret this as the visual difference becoming \emph{additionally} recoverable from text positions over depth, rather than migrating out of the image positions --- a third is still recovered there at layers 18--28. This agrees with prior evidence of image-to-text transfer \citep{basu2024, whatsintheimage2025}.

In the mid band, where both readouts are valid, they disagree about which group dominates: the layer-18 probe gives image $74.9\%$ $[71.3, 78.9]$ vs.\ text $87.4\%$ $[85.9, 88.8]$, while behavioral valence gives image $96.0\%$ $[92.1, 99.6]$ vs.\ text $74.1\%$ $[66.5, 81.6]$. Both orderings are supported by non-overlapping intervals, in opposite directions. Both come from the same model and inference stack, so this reflects \emph{where} the readout is taken --- at layer 18 versus after sixteen further layers of mixing --- not a measurement error. We report both and draw no conclusion about mid-band ordering.

The same broad set of downstream text positions can therefore restore differences caused by either the context or the image. This identifies a plausible meeting point for the two cues, not the computation that resolves their competition. It also does not show whether visual information continues to move into text positions while negative context is winning. In a confirmatory run with fixed negative context, the image-driven valence gap shrinks from $0.87$ to $0.08$. That small denominator makes late-layer recovery unstable, so we could not answer the question. Mid-layer image-position recovery is $85\%$ (CI $[51,150]$), but the wide interval prevents a stronger conclusion.

\subsection{A text-derived direction still moves the answer under conflict}
\label{sec:arbitration}

Finally, we test whether the text-derived direction of \S\ref{sec:foundations} can change a judgment during conflict. On 150 conflict trials, adding the pleasantness direction at layer 18 moves behavioral valence with a slope of $+0.335$ per unit of intervention strength. The earlier no-conflict run gives a similar descriptive slope, $+0.336$, but it is not a matched control and we did not test equivalence; the supported claim is that the intervention remains effective under conflict. Before the sweep, we verified that the direction's length matched the reference value at this internal site (relative error $0.14\%$), guarding against an intervention at the wrong layer output.

The frozen probe readout is flat under this intervention (slope $+0.000$). That is expected rather than a null result: the probe tap sits inside layer 18, upstream of the residual-stream injection at the same layer, so it is invariant to steering by construction. We report it to show the injection is acting where we claim.

This intervention changes the outcome, but it does not show that the competition between the cues changed: a general output-valence direction could shift the final score without changing either cue's weight. Testing whether the intervention changes override rates or cue coefficients is the natural next step.

\section{How far the result travels: models, readouts, and label scoring}
\label{sec:robustness}
\label{sec:models}

Is the asymmetry a property of VLMs in general, or of particular designs? We run the same conflict experiment on four ways of connecting vision to language. \qwen{} uses a native vision transformer accepting varying resolutions. \gemma{} pools its SigLIP output to 256 image tokens. \llava{} passes unpooled patches from a frozen CLIP tower through a linear projection. \llavanext{} uses that same linear-projector design at a far larger token budget (2{,}147 image tokens).

Because each model uses the bounded valence scale differently, no single readout is neutral. We therefore report three: the categorical \emph{override rate} (\S\ref{sec:setup}), the bounded \emph{mirror contrast}, and an \emph{unbounded log-odds margin}, $\log P(\text{pos})/P(\text{neg})$, which removes the scale's bounds entirely. For each we give two intervals: one that resamples photographs only, and one that resamples photographs \emph{and} context sentences together, so that the sentences are treated as a sample rather than as fixed \citep{clark1973, westfall2014}. The second is the stricter test and the one that licenses claims about negative language in general (Table~\ref{tab:models}).

Three results follow.

\textbf{Complete-label scoring changes both LLaVA conclusions.} The original analysis used the first content token for every label. That is complete for Qwen and Gemma here, but not for LLaVA: every LLaVA label begins with a shared prefix token, and several labels contain additional content tokens. Scoring the whole label leaves the trial metadata unchanged but overturns the comparison. \llava{} moves from a null to a strong positive result on all three readouts. \llavanext{} moves in the other direction: its corrected categorical gap is $-11.2\%$, its bounded mirror contrast falls to $+0.125$, and only its unbounded margin retains a crossed interval above zero. The complete-label score is primary because it evaluates the answer requested by the prompt rather than a prefix of that answer.

The text-only control does not explain either result. Without an image, \llava{}'s negative and positive sentences have similar strength (raw unbounded ratio $0.89$). For \llavanext{}, the negative sentences are markedly \emph{weaker} (ratio $0.39$), yet its image-conditioned unbounded contrast is positive. The LLaVA findings therefore are not inherited from stronger negative sentences. A content-token mean gives very different categorical results, but it strongly favors longer labels and is retained only as a sensitivity check (Appendix~\ref{app:contexts}).

\textbf{The readouts disagree, and no clean model boundary remains.} On the varied set, \llava{} clears all three crossed tests, \gemma{} clears the bounded and unbounded tests, \llavanext{} clears only the unbounded test, and \qwen{} clears none. \qwen{}'s support under sentence resampling comes from the matched set of \S\ref{sec:minimal}, where every word pair points the same way. The categorical and graded measures are answering related but different questions: whether the top label crosses a valence category, how probability mass moves on a bounded scale, and how the positive-to-negative odds move without bounds. We therefore report all three rather than choosing the favorable measure for each model.

The neutral correction still matters, especially for \qwen{} and \gemma{}. \qwen{} starts with more negative errors on positive images, while \gemma{} starts with more positive errors on negative images; subtracting those image-specific neutral rates moves \qwen{} down and \gemma{} up. We flag that this categorical correction was applied after seeing the uncorrected results. It follows the neutral-baseline rule already used for the graded readouts, but its effect should not be mistaken for a model property.

\textbf{Two simple architectural explanations do not organize the results.} Both LLaVA models use a frozen CLIP tower and linear projector, and both have positive unbounded contrasts under complete-label scoring. They nevertheless differ sharply on the categorical readout, while also changing language backbone and image-token count. Token count is no simpler: within \qwen{}, a resolution sweep is flat from 128 to 262 image tokens with image discriminability held at AUC $0.983$--$0.986$, while the four cross-model results are non-monotonic. Because tokenizer, backbone, vision tower and connector vary together, we report the architectural variation as unexplained. The most direct connector control, \texttt{llava-v1.6-vicuna-7b}, has not been run (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Mechanism replication.} A coarse version of the replacement experiment on \qwen{} finds that all text positions together restore $65\%$ of the context difference (Appendix~\ref{app:patching}). The individual position groups are non-additive, so we do not assign them causal shares. We did not run mechanism experiments on either LLaVA model.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Where the cues meet.}
The replacement experiments point to the same broad set of downstream text positions for both cues. In the same-image experiment these states restore the context difference; across images they increasingly restore the visual difference over depth. These findings identify a plausible meeting point for image-text conflict. They do not show why negative context has the larger effect. Repeating the intervention separately for neutral-to-positive and neutral-to-negative changes would test whether the asymmetry appears at this stage or is already present upstream.

\paragraph{Why do the models differ?}
We do not know. The complete-label rerun removes the apparent \llava{} null and with it the cleanest architectural contrast. The two LLaVA models share a connector family but differ in backbone, token budget and the size of each readout. Image-token count is non-monotonic across models and flat in the within-\qwen{} sweep. The remaining variation may reflect any of these model differences, or how their probability distributions interact with the readout. The present comparison does not isolate a cause.

\paragraph{Conflicts with stored model knowledge.}
In conflicts between an image and knowledge stored in the model's weights, prior work finds that image tokens carry most of the causal effect \citep{nooralahzadeh2026, lietzow2026}. In our conflicts, the competing text is part of the prompt, and its effect travels through text-token states. Even the image signal becomes recoverable from text positions by the readout. Where a conflict is resolved therefore depends on the type of conflict, the layer, and the model design.

\paragraph{Implications.}
On the affect-sensitive task studied here, a single negative sentence overrides \qwen{}'s visual judgment on $57\%$ of conflict trials against $35\%$ in the mirror direction, once each image's neutral-sentence baseline is subtracted. Uncorrected the same comparison reads $76\%$ against $37\%$. The LLaVA reruns add a second caution: even the sign of the categorical gap can depend on whether a split label is scored as a complete word. We therefore caution against treating any single figure as \emph{the} magnitude of negativity dominance in VLMs. In the tested setup, the result depends on the text's valence, the model, and the decision rule. In \gemma{}, downstream text positions are a useful target for further study because replacing them restores most of the context difference and intervening there changes the output. Generalizing this result to other models requires replication.

\paragraph{Broader impacts and responsible use.}
Inferring emotion from photographs of people is a contested application, and our use of it is diagnostic: we treat the model's emotion judgment as an instrument for studying how it combines modalities, not as evidence that such judgments are valid. Nothing here should be read as support for deploying affect inference on images of people. Three risks follow from the results specifically. \textbf{First,} a system that reads images for affect can have its judgment moved by one accompanying sentence, so a pipeline accepting user-supplied text alongside images inherits a directional source of error. \textbf{Second,} the internal site used for measurement can also be used for intervention: a direction fit on text alone shifts image-conditioned output under conflict (\S\ref{sec:arbitration}). We report it to support scrutiny and monitoring, not as a deployment technique. \textbf{Third,} the resulting error may not be evenly distributed. If negative framing moves judgments farther than positive framing does, people who are more often described in negative terms may absorb more of the error, and we did not measure variation across demographic groups. The photographs are of real people, used under EMOTIC's research access terms; we release index files and context sentences, never images.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Mechanism.} Patching shows where the context difference travels, but not why negative context has the larger effect. We have not separately patched neutral-to-positive and neutral-to-negative changes. The zero recovery from image tokens in the same-image experiment is an arithmetic consequence of prompt order --- the image precedes the context, so those states cannot differ between the two runs --- and says nothing about where visual content is represented. Placing the context before the image would turn that row into a real measurement; we have not run it. Finally, the tokens before the answer may be a general place where prompt information gathers; a matched non-affective control is needed to test whether their role is specific to valence. The varied-set patching sentences differ in token length, so we also plan to repeat the experiment with the token-matched pairs. Under fixed negative context, the image-driven gap becomes too small to localize its path reliably.

\textbf{Statistics.} Our intervals are clustered over the 121 distinct photographs, and we additionally report crossed intervals that resample photographs and context sentences jointly (Table~\ref{tab:models}). The crossed intervals test generalization to new sentences, and with six unrelated events of each valence they are wide and uneven: \llava{} clears all three readouts, \gemma{} two, \llavanext{} one, and \qwen{}, our primary behavioral model, none on the varied set. For \qwen{}, the matched set (\S\ref{sec:minimal}) reduces event variation and brings the mirror contrast to a crossed interval excluding zero ($[+0.11, +0.83]$); all six tested pairs point in the same direction. The baseline-corrected override gap does not clear zero there ($[-2.6, +45.4]$), so the mirror contrast carries that result. What remains unestablished is generalization beyond this \emph{style} of context sentence: every sentence in both sets follows the frame ``This photo was taken \ldots''. Widening the sets across sentence forms, not just adding more of the same frame, is the highest-value follow-up and requires no new method. One image appears in both polarity groups; removing it changes the matched-set mirror contrast by $0.008$. All runs use a single seed.

\textbf{Measurement.} Behavioral valence is bounded between $-1$ and $1$, and models use that range very differently: \qwen{} saturates it while \gemma{} sits near the ceiling on positive images. We therefore report an override rate and an unbounded log-odds margin alongside it. Comparing only the first content token produced qualitatively wrong LLaVA results; the paper now scores complete labels. Averaging instead of summing the token scores rewards longer labels in these runs (length-score correlations $0.93$ for \llava{} and $0.91$ for \llavanext{}) and collapses nearly every trial onto a negative label. The fixed 13-label list also remains vulnerable to unoffered synonyms \citep{holtzman2021}. EMOTIC labels people within images, whereas we provide the whole image without marking the target person; a rerun with marked or cropped people is the direct fix. The conflict runs, behavioral re-scores, and mechanism interventions reported here use the reference HuggingFace implementations. The original text-only probe training and caption-control states used TransformerBridge; the text path and corresponding HuggingFace activation site were checked directly, and the main image-readout result was rerun through HuggingFace ($\rho=0.510$, AUC $0.912$). Two further experiments --- a six-way rephrasing sweep and a within-family scale comparison --- used a wrapper path that changed the multimodal forward pass, so we withdraw their results. Question phrasing therefore remains untested.

\textbf{Scope.} We study one dataset, one task family (person-emotion labeling), one context style, and four model designs. The probe's validation $r^2$ selected its layer and is not an untouched test estimate; the downstream EMOTIC evaluation is the independent transfer test. The mechanism analysis is detailed for \gemma{} and coarse for \qwen{} because the frozen text-trained probe was fit on \gemma{} activations. The behavioral comparison does not identify why models differ, and the LLaVA correction shows why post-hoc architectural stories are risky. Replicating the full replacement analysis on \qwen{} would require refitting that probe. Our claims therefore concern controlled image-context emotion judgments in the tested models. Other conflict domains, tasks, and architectures remain open.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Flipping a single valence word in an otherwise identical sentence, with the photograph and event held fixed, moves \qwen{}'s image-grounded emotion judgment four to five times farther when the word is negative than when it is positive, and all six tested pairs agree. With no image, those pairs show no detectable mean difference; this argues against a large wording imbalance but cannot exclude a small one. On a varied set of unrelated events the estimate is less precise and does not survive resampling the events. In \gemma{}, replacing downstream text-position states restores $88$--$93\%$ of the context difference, and those positions restore more of the visual difference later in the network; a direction estimated only from text still moves the output during conflict. Across four models, no architectural boundary emerges. Which measure registers the effect varies by model, and scoring only the first piece of a split emotion word creates an apparent null in one model and reverses the categorical ordering of another. The central result is a controlled valence asymmetry in the tested setup, with a plausible meeting point for the cues rather than a universal architectural law.

\subsection{Combining directions (supporting evidence)}
In a 30-image pilot, combinations of text-derived appraisal directions produce specific emotions predicted by appraisal theory. Neither component alone produces anger: unpleasantness alone favors fear, while other-responsibility with its valence component removed favors joy. Combining the two at matched norm makes anger the top emotion on $97\%$ of images. This matches the appraisal prediction \emph{anger = negative + other-caused}. \section{Context sentences, prompt, and label set}
\label{app:contexts}

\paragraph{Label set.}
The 13 allowed emotion labels are anger, boredom, disgust, fear, guilt, joy, pride, relief, sadness, shame, surprise, trust, and neutral. Behavioral valence is the total probability of \{joy, pride, relief, trust\} minus the total probability of \{anger, boredom, disgust, fear, guilt, sadness, shame\}. Surprise and neutral contribute to neither side. This partition matches the released code.

\paragraph{Scoring split labels.}
LLaVA tokenizes every label into a shared prefix token followed by one to three content tokens. We sum the conditional log probabilities of every token in the label; the shared prefix adds the same constant to all 13 scores and therefore does not affect their normalized probabilities. The legacy first-content-token score changes \llava{} from a strong asymmetry to a null and changes \llavanext{} from one crossed test clearing zero to all three. We also tested averaging the content-token scores, which might appear to correct for word length. It does not: the mean score remains strongly correlated with token count ($r=0.93$ for \llava{}, $0.91$ for \llavanext{}) and makes nearly every trial select a negative label. We therefore use the complete-label sum and disclose the other two rules only as sensitivity checks.

\paragraph{Varied set.}
Table~\ref{tab:contextbank} lists the varied set verbatim, with each sentence's strength when run with no image and its mean behavioral valence on the positive-image group, both for \qwen{}. The text-only column is in log-odds rather than bounded valence because \qwen{} pins the bounded score to $\pm 1$ with no image present (Appendix~\ref{app:contexts}, above), which would make every sentence look identical. One negative sentence is markedly weaker than the rest in isolation (n1, $-4.9$ against $-11$ to $-20$ for the others) and is also the only negative sentence that fails to move the judgment on positive images ($+0.65$ against a neutral baseline of $+0.63$). The negative sentences are therefore not uniformly extreme, and the effect does not depend on any single one of them. The two neutral sentences also differ from each other ($+0.91$ and $+0.34$), which is why every effect in the paper is measured against their mean rather than against one of them.

\paragraph{The matched set.}
Each of the six pairs keeps the sentence structure fixed and changes only the valence-bearing word. The two sentences in every pair have equal token counts under both the Qwen tokenizer used for the headline result and the Gemma tokenizer used during stimulus development. The readout position is therefore identical within each pair. The exact sentences appear below, with the positive member first; each begins ``This photo was taken \ldots'':

  \item[mp1] \ldots on the \{best $\mid$ worst\} day of their life.
  \item[mp2] \ldots moments after they \{got $\mid$ lost\} the job they wanted.
  \item[mp3] \ldots moments after they heard \{wonderful $\mid$ devastating\} news.
  \item[mp4] \ldots at a \{celebration $\mid$ memorial\} for a close friend.
  \item[mp5] \ldots during a \{joyful $\mid$ heartbreaking\} moment.
\end{itemize}
\paragraph{Do the sentences differ in isolation?}
The asymmetry could be a property of the sentences rather than of the conflict: if the negative members are simply the stronger stimuli, they would dominate whatever the image showed. We test this directly by running every sentence with no image at all.

On \qwen{} the bounded valence readout cannot answer the question. With no image the model reads even a neutral sentence as sadness ($-0.996$), so $87\%$ of matched-set rows sit on a scale bound and the ratio comes out at exactly $1.00$ by construction --- a number produced by two saturated constants, not by the sentences. We therefore read this control on the unbounded log-odds margin (\S\ref{sec:models}), which has no bounds to pin against.

On that scale the six matched pairs show no detectable mean difference. The pooled ratio is $|\text{neg}|/|\text{pos}|=1.02$; per pair it ranges from $0.67$ to $1.22$ with four of six leaning negative, and the mean paired difference is $+0.33$ log-odds (Wilcoxon $p=0.69$, $n=6$ pairs). The varied set gives $0.94$. With an image present the mean paired difference is $+10.46$ log-odds, and all six pairs point in the same direction (Wilcoxon $p=0.031$). The contrast between these controls argues against a large text-only imbalance as the source of the image-conditioned result.

Three caveats. Six pairs give little power, so this bounds a gross imbalance rather than excluding a small one. The control is per-model. Under complete-label scoring, the varied-set raw log-odds ratios are $0.89$ for \llava{} and $0.39$ for \llavanext{}: in both cases the negative sentences are no stronger than the positives without an image. Their neutral baselines are positive ($+0.29$ and $+1.48$ log-odds), while \qwen{} and \gemma{} lean negative without an image. Because a neutral sentence is not a common zero point across models, stimulus-strength ratios here are taken against zero rather than against the neutral sentence.

The earlier \gemma{} figures of $1.06$ (varied) and $1.25$ (matched) were measured on the superseded inference stack and on the bounded readout. They reproduce exactly from those files and we record them as provenance, but they do not bear on the numbers above.

\paragraph{Prompt.}
The semantic prompt is identical across models, but each uses its native chat template. The Gemma form is shown here; the context part is omitted in the no-context condition:
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
<start\_of\_turn>user\textbackslash n<start\_of\_image>Context: \{ctx\} What single emotion is this person feeling?<end\_of\_turn>\textbackslash n<start\_of\_turn>model\textbackslash n
\end{quote}
The readout position is the final prompt token, computed as \texttt{input\_ids.shape[-1] - 1}, never a hardcoded index.

\section{Where in depth the context signal appears}
\label{app:layerwise}

Where in the network do positive- and negative-context runs begin to differ? At each layer we project the last-token state onto the frozen probe direction --- reading a fixed direction off every depth, in the manner of the logit lens and its successors \citep{nostalgebraist2020, belrose2023}, as a diagnostic rather than a re-fitted probe. We summarize the gap between the two context polarities as a paired effect size over images, $d(L)$, which removes the growth in residual-stream norm with depth. The run covers 60 positive-group images; $51$ of them yield a complete positive/negative pair at every layer and enter the paired statistic.

The separation first clears the pre-divergence noise floor at layer 12 of 34, and it arrives already large: $|d| = 3.9$ there, against a floor set at three times the median absolute separation over the first eight layers. It does not then amplify --- $|d|$ is $2.0$ at the readout layer 18 and $3.0$ at layer 28, with no late peak. In \emph{raw} projection units the separation does grow by more than an order of magnitude over the same span ($-0.40$ at layer 12 to $-6.10$ at layer 28), but that growth tracks the residual-stream norm rather than the consistency of the effect, which is why we report the scale-free form. We therefore locate the entry of the context signal in the mid band and make no claim that it strengthens with depth. This shows where the difference becomes legible to this probe; it does not show where the signal is created.

The context's effect on the final token need not be direct: the final token could read the context words itself, or the context could first reshape other positions that the final token then reads. The replacement experiment in \S\ref{sec:patching} tests which downstream positions are sufficient to restore the difference.

\section{Correcting for the scale's bounds}
\label{app:headroom}

The valence score is bounded in $[-1,1]$, so changes can be compressed near either end. The main analysis removes the bounds with the log-odds score of \S\ref{sec:setup}. As a secondary check, we also divide each shift by the room remaining in the direction it moves.

Under that correction the asymmetry persists in \qwen{} (drop $0.714$ vs.\ rise $0.394$ on the varied set; $0.924$ vs.\ $0.518$ on the matched set) and in complete-label \llava{} (drop $0.643$ vs.\ rise $0.152$). It is weaker in \llavanext{} (drop $0.339$ vs.\ rise $0.263$), matching the ordering of the complete-label graded scores. We prefer the log-odds score in the main text because dividing by remaining room is unstable when the denominator is small: \gemma{}'s positive-image baseline sits at $+0.905$, close enough to the ceiling that the ratio becomes meaningless. We do not report a head-room-corrected number for \gemma{} for that reason.

\section{The no-context baseline confound}
\label{app:nocontext}

All analyses exclude the no-context condition and measure every effect against the neutral-sentence baseline. The reason is primarily a design one: the no-context prompt differs from every experimental condition in a way that has nothing to do with valence, namely whether a sentence is present at all. Baselining against a neutral sentence holds that difference fixed so the manipulation is purely polarity.

The empirical difference between the two baselines is small in the models we can measure it on (Table~\ref{tab:nocontext}): adding a neutral sentence shifts positive-image valence by $+0.059$ in \qwen{} and by $-0.011$ in \gemma{}. We note this explicitly because an earlier version of this appendix reported a much larger shift ($+0.411$) together with a matching move in the frozen probe readout, and concluded that the effect was representational. Those measurements came from the superseded inference stack described in \S\ref{sec:limitations}; the probe column is not available on the current stack, and we withdraw the representational claim. The design argument above does not depend on it.

\section{Pilot vs.\ varied set}
\label{app:pilot}

A 40-image pilot used one context sentence of each valence. It suggested that the image dominated (ratio $0.15$--$0.22$) and that the valence asymmetry was very large. Both conclusions depended on one weak positive sentence, and neither survived moving to the full sentence set. We keep this appendix for the methodological point --- a conflict experiment resting on a single context sentence per valence is measuring that sentence --- and not for its numbers, which were produced on the superseded inference stack (\S\ref{sec:limitations}) and are not comparable with anything reported in the main text.

\section{Patching methodology}
\label{app:patching}

\paragraph{Same-image design (\S\ref{sec:patching}).}
For each image, the positive-context run supplies activations and the negative-context run receives them. We overwrite \texttt{resid\_post} across layers 13--17 for one aligned token group at a time. At the layer-18 probe, recovery is $(\text{patched} - \text{negative})/(\text{positive} - \text{negative})$. The groups are the 256 image tokens, question tokens, the beginning-of-sequence token, user-turn prefix markers, and the assistant-turn suffix (\texttt{<end\_of\_turn>\textbackslash n<start\_of\_turn>model}). The final scoring token is excluded from every group. The first, third and fourth of these groups lie before the context, and the image is identical in the two runs, so their donor and recipient states coincide and their recovery is $0\%$ by construction rather than by measurement; they serve as a check on the position alignment.

We use 60 images for each of two sentence pairs: championship/funeral and wonderful/devastating. Recovery uses the same ratio-of-sums estimator as the cross-image design, bootstrapped over the 60 images with $2{,}000$ resamples; \texttt{src/experiments/patching\_intervals.py} recomputes every interval in Tables~\ref{tab:patching} and~\ref{tab:crosspatch} from the saved per-row outputs. Behavioral-valence recovery follows the same ordering as probe recovery but is noisier; all-text recovery is $73$--$78\%$. The varied-set sentences differ in token length, so matched groups occur at different absolute positions. A planned rerun with token-matched pairs removes this positional confound.

\paragraph{Cross-image design (\S\ref{sec:crosspatch}).}
The positive-image run supplies activations and the negative-image run receives them, while the context stays fixed. Their \texttt{input\_ids} are identical, so every text position can be matched exactly. We use 60 image pairs and patch three layer ranges: 0--12, 13--17, and 18--28. Recovery is a ratio of sums, $\sum(\text{patched} - \text{neg}) / \sum(\text{pos} - \text{neg})$, rather than a mean of per-row ratios, because an individual pair's denominator can sit near zero; its interval is a percentile bootstrap over the 60 pairs with $2{,}000$ resamples. Behavioral valence is the main recovery measure. The layer-18 probe provides a check where valid; because its activation is computed from layer 17, it cannot measure recovery caused by patching layers 18--28. Patching every position except the scoring token recovers $99.9\%$, $100.0\%$, and $89.9\%$ across the three ranges. Under negative context, the image-driven gap shrinks to $0.08$, making the late-range denominator nearly zero. We report those confidence intervals but do not interpret them.

\paragraph{Qwen port.}
For Qwen, we patch through the standard HuggingFace implementation directly and score recovery on behavioral valence on 60 positive images across a broad middle-to-late layer range. Question tokens recover $12\%$, the turn markers recover $6\%$, and all aligned text positions together recover $65\%$. The joint recovery is much larger than the sum of the two individual estimates, showing that these interventions are non-additive; we therefore do not interpret the individual values as causal shares. Image positions again recover none, and again that is forced by prompt order rather than measured (\S\ref{sec:patching}).

\section{Compute}
\label{app:compute}

The conflict and intervention experiments use inference-only forward passes in bf16 on one A100-class GPU with 40 GB of memory. They use no gradients or answer generation; the separate caption control generated descriptions for 1{,}000 images. Each model's varied-set base run is 150 person-annotations $\times$ 15 conditions $=$ 2{,}250 forward passes, and the \qwen{} matched-pair run is another 2{,}250; four models are run, each with a 15-pass text-only control. The conflict intervention sweep is 150 conflict trials $\times$ 7 strengths $=$ 1{,}050 passes. Layerwise localization is 60 positive-group images $\times$ 14 contexts $=$ 840 passes, each cached at every layer in a single forward. Same-image replacement covers 60 images $\times$ 2 sentence pairs $\times$ (7 position groups $+$ 2 baselines); cross-image replacement covers 60 image pairs $\times$ 3 layer bands $\times$ (6 position groups $+$ 2 baselines). The \qwen{} resolution sweep repeats the base run at three image resolutions. Counts here cover the reported experiments; two withdrawn subsections consumed a further 13{,}500 and 4{,}500 passes. \todo{Add wall-clock totals per run from the session logs for camera-ready; the full project including preliminary and failed runs used more compute than the reported experiments.}
